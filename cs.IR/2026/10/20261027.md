### ACOR: incremental evidence accumulation towards real-time DNA storage reconstruction
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36500v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.IR**, q-bio.GN
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.36500v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-27 01:58:03+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-27 01:58:03+00:00
- **Authors**: Linxuan Han, Huaming Wu, Shi Jia, Guanjin Qu
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: DNA offers high-density, durable storage by encoding digital information into synthetic strands and retrieving it through sequencing. Sequence reconstruction combines noisy copies of each strand before downstream decoding. Conventional offline methods typically reconstruct completed read clusters, separating reconstruction from ongoing read collection. Streaming reconstruction offers a way to overlap evidence processing with sequencing and reduce the computation remaining before downstream decoding. We present ACOR, a streaming consensus reconstruction method that records $k$-mer transitions and boundary support in a persistent ledger, then decodes through length-constrained beam search and priority-based selection at cluster closure. Evaluation on real and simulated DNA-storage datasets showed competitive recovery. On our own short-read next-generation sequencing (NGS) wet-lab library, ACOR combined near-complete recovery with second-scale reconstruction of more than ten million reads. A smaller public benchmark was completed in a fraction of a second. Parallel ACOR achieved speedups of tens to over a thousand-fold relative to the tested serial baseline configurations. Sparse storage reduced high-context memory requirements, while parallel execution accelerated reconstruction computation. ACOR is designed for streaming readout pipelines that process assigned reads while sequencing continues.



### Monitor Accuracy Isn't the Answer: A Decision-Value Framework for Evaluating AI Agent Restart Monitors
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36544v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.AI**, cs.IR
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.36544v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-27 02:59:21+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-27 02:59:21+00:00
- **Authors**: Siddharth Jain, Venkat N. Vedam
- **Comment**: 29 pages, 6 figures, 14 tables
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Classification accuracy and true-positive and false-positive rates alone do not determine whether restarting an AI agent improves success. Using an exact alarm-to-action identity for an independent clean restart with zero monitoring overhead, we show analytically that a monitor with fixed 90% accuracy can swing from a five-percentage-point loss to a 35-point gain in success rate, depending solely on the replacement attempt's residual-budget success probability. We introduce two techniques for evaluating monitors and progress signals in decision terms. First, a decision-sufficiency criterion, a binary-action specialization of Blackwell's comparison of experiments, identifies which progress representations or context compressions preserve the information needed to choose optimally between the specified actions, independent of trace reconstruction fidelity. Second, a finite-sample, model-free decision audit uses simultaneous binomial intervals over existing agent traces, without additional model calls, to certify a preference for "restart" or "continue" under stated sampling assumptions, or return "abstain" when evidence is insufficient. In a study of 194 externally evaluated model executions on 19 selected SymPy issues from SWE-bench Verified, with a retrospective audit of 130 of these traces, we find a 2.29-point apparent restart gain traced to a single surviving failed attempt, a 42.29-point loss on a second model, no positive replication of the initial gain on prospective task cohorts, and zero of 103 live comparisons meeting the specified certification bar. These results motivate evaluating agent monitors through recovery opportunity, decision value, and statistical support, rather than classification performance alone.



### QESChunker: A Single Objective Unifies Overlapping and Non-Overlapping Chunking for RAG

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.36557v1)

- **Authors**: Yifan Zhao, Qianyang Zhao, Xinyu Guan, kai wei, Yuming Deng
- **Comment**: 16 pages, 8 figures, 4 tables
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Chunking is a fundamental preprocessing step in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), yet most existing chunkers rely primarily on heuristics. We introduce the Question--Evidence-Supervised Chunker (QESChunker), to our knowledge the first method for constructing static chunk indices from question--evidence supervision. QESChunker constructs document-specific chunk indices by optimizing over human-annotated or LLM-generated question--evidence pairs, without access to held-out test queries. We formulate non-overlapping and overlapping index construction through a unified question--evidence utility-maximization objective, turning chunking into a supervised combinatorial optimization problem. Under the non-overlap constraint, the objective decomposes over chunks and can be optimized exactly in polynomial time by dynamic programming. When overlap is allowed, the objective becomes monotone submodular; exact optimization is NP-hard, but our greedy achieves a $(1-1/\sqrt{e})$-approximation. Across two self-constructed and three public datasets, both variants outperform existing chunking methods in complete-evidence recall in nearly all evaluated settings, under the same overlap regime and a fixed hybrid retriever. These results recast chunking as supervised, budget-aware index construction for evidence retrieval.

### Recall or Relearn? A Mixture of In-Context Experts for Recurring Concept Drift
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36585v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.LG**, cs.IR
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.36585v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-27 03:42:18+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-27 03:42:18+00:00
- **Authors**: Zhangkai Wu, Xu Zhang, Zhihong Cui, Yiqi Wang, Yi Zhang
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Tabular foundation models (TFMs) are being applied to Web data streams such as network intrusion detection, where they adapt by updating the labelled rows of their context without retraining. When a past concept returns after leaving the context, a TFM faces a recall-or-relearn decision between restoring stored rows of the concept and relearning it from new labels. We propose MICE (Mixture of In-Context Experts), a training-free learner that cuts the stream into segments at detected changes. It merges a closed segment into a stored context when the TFM's estimated additional error on the segment is within a tolerance, under a capacity limit on stored contexts. A fast level weights the stored contexts and first-in-first-out (FIFO) windows by the log-loss of their predictions on the last labelled batch. We show that, with pure stored contexts and a margin condition, this fast level gains over relearning, in expected accuracy, the normalised positive area between the stored context's accuracy and the TFM's learning curve, up to a first-batch term. A slow level mixes the fast level's prediction with that of the FIFO window of the last labelled rows and bounds the discounted half-Brier regret of MICE against this window at every time. Learning curves measured beforehand predict the ordering of the observed recall gains. MICE outperforms reset, which empties the context at detected drifts, on grid streams with random boundary phases. Merging keeps fewer experts than pools that store every segment, and with fixed segmentation a time-matched snapshot pool matches MICE on the shifted grid. On real streams, MICE has the highest mean accuracy of all policies we compare.

- **Summary**: A user history can admit multiple plausible next interactions, yet conventional sequential recommenders produce a single deterministic ranking. While diffusion models offer a natural way to sample alternative continuations, we show that existing target-item diffusion recommenders often suffer from severe sampling collapse, producing nearly identical outputs across independent draws. - **Summary**: Coordinating multiple recommendation objectives (e.g., click and conversion) is critical for real-world generative recommendation (GR). - **Summary**: Food similarity underlies retrieval, recommendation, substitution, and other food computing tasks, yet food representations are typically evaluated by identity preservation. - **Summary**: Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used as recommendation generators in conversational recommender systems (RS) via retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). This flexible design introduces new sources of structural bias. This paper studies positional bias in top-1 and single-turn LLM-based RS: systematic dependence of the recommended item on the order in which candidates are presented in the prompt. We propose two complementary detection methodologies: (i) POB-ICM, based on contingency matrix analysis and Chi-square independence testing, and (ii) POB-UNBP, a sample-efficient approach derived from a uniformity null model under permutation-based evaluation, enabling stochastic decoding with formal statistical guarantees. Using a controlled RAG testbed for product recommendation, built on a real product catalog, a fixed prompt template and 2000 template-generated user queries with 20 permutations each (40,000 instances), both methodologies reject the null hypothesis of positional independence and uniformity in all evaluated state-of-the-art models. We introduce three complementary metrics -- permutation sensitivity, positional bias, and strong positional bias -- to quantify this effect, the latter targeting cases where the position displaces the recommendation towards clearly less relevant items rather than only choosing among comparably relevant ones. A robustness analysis across candidate-set size and decoding temperature shows that the bias is structural and model-dependent, not an artifact of deterministic decoding or candidate-set size. These results establish positional bias as a measurable, reproducible property of prompt-mediated recommendation pipelines in the evaluated setting and motivate routine auditing and governance controls (e.g., order randomization, standardized prompting) for deployed LLM-based RS. Source code is publicly released.

- **Summary**: LLM-based generative recommendation reads long, text-rich interaction histories but generates only short semantic identifiers (SIDs). This asymmetry makes supervised fine-tuning inefficient: encoding the history is expensive, yet each history usually provides supervision for only one target. We present Merge and Branch (M&B) to reduce input cost and learn more from each encoded history. Merge compresses each historical item's SID and text into a small number of learned tokens. Branch uses the compressed history to predict multiple target SIDs. To share history computation while keeping these targets independent, M&B organizes each example as a shallow, wide tree. We develop tree FlashAttention to execute this layout efficiently, processing the shared history once and propagating gradients from every branch for end-to-end training. A tree-structured key/value cache extends history sharing to constrained decoding. Experiments on public benchmarks show that M&B outperforms strong recommendation baselines while substantially improving training and decoding efficiency.
