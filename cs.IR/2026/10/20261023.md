### From Item Scores to Semantic Tokens: Test-Time Collaborative Adaptation for Generative Recommendation
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32098v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.IR**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.32098v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-23 03:01:12+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-23 03:01:12+00:00
- **Authors**: Xiongfeng Shan, Xing Tang, Hanchong Chen, Fuyuan Lyu, Lingjie Li, Jingyan Jiang, Duguang Liu, Xiuqiang He
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Generative recommenders using semantic identifiers predict items through successive token decisions, but beam search may irreversibly prune promising item paths. This limitation is particularly consequential when a few recent interactions signal an emerging interest while earlier behavior favors established preferences. Yet an independently trained discriminative recommender may rank the same items highly from the same interaction history. Such complementary scores are available at test time, offering a way to adapt a frozen generator without retraining either model. The challenge is to use these scores before pruning: combining completed recommendation lists cannot alter earlier beam decisions, while scores over complete items cannot directly inform choices among semantic prefixes shared by multiple items. We propose \textbf{T}est-Time \textbf{C}ollaborative \textbf{B}eam \textbf{S}earch \textbf{A}daptation (\textbf{TCBSA}), a training-free method that incorporates collaborative information into semantic decoding. TCBSA transforms discriminative score margins into potentials over shared semantic edges and uses a confidence gate to regulate their contributions to cumulative path scores before beam selection. We establish a sufficient condition for target prefix survival under global beam competition and bound score perturbations under low discriminative confidence. Full-catalog experiments show consistent improvements over strong baselines across datasets and different generative and discriminative recommenders. Analyses of emerging interests and decoding behavior demonstrate gains in recommendation accuracy and target path survival, while targeted interventions support the contribution of shared prefix corrections. TCBSA also offers a favorable balance between recommendation accuracy and inference latency compared with simply widening the native beam.

- **Comment**: None

- **Summary**: Existing personalized information retrieval methods mainly model differences in user needs through user preferences and query intent, while paying little attention to the personalized expression differences that arise when different users express the same retrieval need. Existing real query data typically lack stable user identities, long-term expression histories, and intent-matched counterparts, making it difficult to systematically evaluate the impact of personalized expression differences on retrieval outcomes. To this end, we propose PersonalQuery-Benchmark (PQB). PQB provides a new benchmark and evaluation framework for systematically studying personalized expression robustness. ### System One models Are Not Better Rerankers
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32318v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.IR
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.32318v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-23 08:16:13+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-23 08:16:13+00:00
- **Authors**: Michael Dinzinger, Kanishka Ghosh Dastidar, Michael Granitzer
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: System One models, or decision models, answer a question about an input with a calibrated probability instead of generated text. Within weeks of the first release, in September 2026, they were used as rerankers. We test whether the decision fine-tuning behind them makes a better reranker than plain reranker fine-tuning of the same model. For six Qwen backbones from 0.6B to 27B parameters and ten TREC DL and BEIR collections, we compare the untuned model, asked zero-shot, with its decision fine-tunes and its reranker fine-tunes, two of which we train ourselves. In our work, we draw the conclusion that decision fine-tuning never makes the better reranker. From 0.6B to 9B, reranker fine-tuning wins on every backbone by 5 to 29 points nDCG@10, also on zero-shot collections, and at 27B the two tie. Up to 9B, decision fine-tuning adds nothing over simply asking the untuned model. Moreover, we find that the calibration advantage of decision models depends on the question. Asked whether a passage answers the query, they are better calibrated than the published reranker fine-tunes from 4B on. Asked a slightly different question from prior work, they lose this advantage. Lastly, calibration does not make up for a worse ranking: up to 9B, a reranker fine-tune keeps the better passages at a fixed threshold. If you need a reranker, fine-tune a reranker.

- **DOI**: None

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.32336v1)

- **Authors**: Tianyi Sun, Pengfei Yao, Yanze Wang, Hongda Chen, Yunyun Han, Jun Sun, Chuanrui Hu, Yafeng Deng
- **Comment**: 12 pages, 3 figures. Code: https://github.com/EverMind-AI/SkillCorpus/tree/main/skillrep
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Retrieving the right skill from a large library is essential for LLM agents, yet requests and skills are written for different purposes and in different words. Existing retrievers narrow this request-skill gap with one or two representation choices each, so it is unknown whether their gains hold in combination. No prior work measures the gap itself, so it is also unclear which part of it each choice narrows. We define a representation design space by five choices -- encoder fine-tuning, query-side and document-side expansion, background projection and a lexical channel -- and evaluate them together for the first time, as modules of one retriever, SkillRep. With a 0.6B encoder and LLM expansion, SkillRep improves MRR@10 on SRA-Bench over the strongest same-size retriever by 7.02 points with the official skill pool and 8.79 with UniPool, a 111,247-skill pool we build from public registries, and is best on SkillsBench. The value of each choice depends on the others. Fine-tuning, for instance, reverses the ranking of the four training-free modules and turns them from complementary into overlapping. To understand why, we measure the gap with three quantities: background, the embedding component that does not separate skills; unsaid vocabulary, the gold skill's content a request leaves unsaid; and dilution, the information lost when a skill is encoded into a single vector. They show which part each module narrows and by what mechanism. This reveals that modules narrowing the same part compete for it, while those narrowing different parts complement each other.

### Knowledge Distillation across Item and Code Spaces for Parallel Generative Recommendation
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32943v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.IR**, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.32943v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-23 17:37:55+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-23 17:37:55+00:00
- **Authors**: Yixuan Xu, Hailin Liu, Linxiang Peng, Can Yang
- **Comment**: 12 pages, 2 figures
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Generative recommendation with semantic IDs represents each item as a sequence of discrete codes derived from its semantic representation and predicts these codes to generate recommendations. Existing semantic-ID recommenders are typically trained with hard labels for the observed next item. Although such supervision identifies the target, it provides no explicit distributional information about alternative items conditioned on the same user history. Sequential recommenders can supply this item-level predictive knowledge, but transferring it to semantic-ID generation is challenging because the teacher and student operate in item and code spaces, respectively. We propose two complementary knowledge-distillation objectives for parallel semantic-ID recommenders, which predict all code positions simultaneously without autoregressive prefix conditioning. Item Distribution Matching (IDM) reconstructs a catalogue-level item distribution from the student's parallel code predictions and aligns it with the teacher's item distribution. Code Marginal Matching (CMM) projects the teacher's item probabilities onto each code position using fixed item-to-code assignments, then matches the resulting code marginals to the corresponding student heads. Both objectives transfer the teacher's soft distributional knowledge while preserving the student's parallel prediction structure. Experiments on benchmark datasets show that both methods consistently outperform existing state-of-the-art approaches. On average, IDM improves Recall@10 by 15.39%, while CMM improves NDCG@10 by 8.28%. These results demonstrate that distillation in either reconstructed item space or code-marginal space can substantially strengthen hard-label supervision for parallel generative recommendation.



### Separating Thinking from Spelling for Efficient Generative Recommendation
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32980v2
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.IR**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.32980v2)
- **Published**: 2026-10-23 17:59:24+00:00

- **Authors**: Danil Gusak, Anna Volodkevich, Ivan Oseledets, Evgeny Frolov
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Generative recommenders retrieve items by writing their semantic IDs (SIDs) code by code under trie-constrained beam search, an approach now deployed on large web platforms. Standard autoregressive interfaces pay for the SID length K twice: a history of T items enters the backbone as TK code tokens, and every beam-search step runs through the backbone, so returning B candidates costs O(BK) backbone decode tokens per user. The second cost often dominates, and it grows with the backbone, the SID depth and the candidate budget.   We introduce Deco (decoding from concepts), which separates reading the history from writing the identifier. The backbone reads each history item as one fused token and condenses the history once into a compact next-item concept; a light KV-cached decoder conditioned on the concept and the generated prefix runs the entire beam search, while the identifiers, the trie and the autoregressive factorization stay unchanged. The decoder approaches full-backbone accuracy with cross-entropy alone, and distillation improves it further. On seven datasets from e-commerce, movies and music streaming, Deco achieves comparable or better accuracy than both a standard generative recommender and the full-backbone teacher with fused inputs, while delivering 3.2-8.6$\times$ their throughput under a 50 ms p95 latency budget in a production-style serving pipeline. Delegating beam search makes larger candidate pools and models practical: Deco generates 1000 candidates faster than its teacher generates 100, and with a 1.5B-parameter LLM backbone, it matches the 69M-parameter teacher's throughput while achieving higher accuracy.

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.33224v1)

- **Summary**: In the age of AI, privacy-preserving approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search over high-dimensional embeddings has emerged as a critical yet frustratingly challenging domain, dominated by tradeoffs between speed, privacy, and accuracy. Traditional tree and graph-based indexing structures (e.g., HNSW, ScaNN) rely fundamentally on non-linear operations that are computationally intractable under Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE) or blind server-side constraints.   In this paper, we introduce Holo-NN (Holographic Nearest Neighbor Search), an algebraic framework that replaces data-dependent branching with a single linear projection. Our key observation is that while distances across a database are dense, neighborhood membership is inherently sparse: only a handful of points ever lie close to a query. By pairing locality-preserving feature transforms with Compressive Sensing, Holo-NN folds the entire database offline into a compact holographic sensing matrix. At query time, encrypted search reduces to a single, non-interactive matrix-vector multiplication---requiring no FHE bootstrapping, no multi-round interaction, and no client-side index state. This turns private retrieval into an embarrassingly parallel, accuracy-tunable computation that runs out of the box on both CPUs and GPUs, scaling efficiently to production databases. We offer a comparison against other state-of-the-art Private ANN algorithms to help situate the method in the broader context of production-grade private retrieval.
