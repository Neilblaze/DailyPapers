- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39090v1
- **DOI**: None

- **Authors**: Sizhang Zhao, Praveen Gondhi, Bin Li, Utsaw Kumar
- **Comment**: Accepted to GRAIL @ CIKM 2026. 5 pages, 2 figures, 3 tables
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Local-commerce and food-delivery marketplaces run large ads businesses: across hundreds of thousands of stores, merchants pay to be surfaced on browse and search surfaces, and a ranker decides which ads each consumer sees. Ranking well demands strong per-consumer personalization, yet a consumer's raw interaction history is sparse and high-cardinality. Our production ranker had personalized only through hand-engineered signals: aggregate engagement counts, categorical embeddings, and order-agnostic pooled history, with no learned per-consumer sequence representation. We learn one by training a GPT-style generative model of the consumer's engagement sequence to predict their next few engagements, and read its last-token hidden state as the consumer embedding. That single frozen vector becomes one shared personalization input for every ad surface: a daily job refreshes it, and each ranker consumes it as an ordinary dense feature. Offline it is the largest single-feature gain in our ranking-AUC ladder, a relative Eval-AUC gain of +0.59% on a browse-driven feed and +0.57% on a query-driven search surface. Online, it shipped as one component of bundled ranker retrains on both surfaces: Discovery (revenue +0.79%, conversion rate +1.17%) and Search (revenue +0.94%, conversion rate +1.50%). Three design choices adapt the generative objective to a repurchase-heavy marketplace, and are the core of our contribution: an exploration-weighted loss that up-weights businesses new to the consumer over ones already engaged; a two-stage curriculum: a click pretrain that keeps the representation broad and exploratory, then a conversion fine-tune that sharpens purchase intent; and a designed negative set that pairs a fixed popular-business pool and businesses shown but not ordered with random and in-batch negatives.



### PRISM: Personalized Item Representations in User History
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39175v1

- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Sequential recommenders typically construct history inputs from item identifiers or content features using an embedding map shared across users. The same item therefore yields the same initial embedding representation, even when users have different relationships to the item. We propose personalized item representations in user history, where each item's embedding expresses its relationship to the user. Personalization becomes a property of the history inputs themselves, supplying richer evidence for later sequence modeling.   We instantiate this idea with PRISM, which reuses upstream ranking computation to generate personalized item embeddings. An autoencoder jointly compresses all layer states from a user tower, a user--item tower, and same-request candidate mixing into one embedding per scored pair for downstream replay. Offline evaluation on Surface 1, a major recommendation surface at billion scale, reports an average downstream normalized entropy (NE) change of $-0.15\%$, compared with $-0.07\%$ for VISTA at similar history lengths. We further identify an empirical sequence-length scaling law: over history lengths of 26--240 events, NE gain follows a fitted power law with exponent 0.468, increasing from 0.06\% to 0.17\%. Therefore, personalized item representations in user history provide a new scaling dimension for improving recommendation quality.



### Read More, Reason Deeper: Latent Workspaces for Long-Sequence Recommendation
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39188v1

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.39188v1)

- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Long-sequence recommendation can improve along two axes: \emph{evidence scaling} exposes longer histories, while \emph{reasoning scaling} adds layers that interpret that evidence for a candidate. Existing models couple them: sequence encoders propagate every added event through depth, whereas one-shot selection prevents later layers from revisiting omitted evidence. We introduce the Latent Workspace Ranker (LWR), which keeps typed evidence in persistent banks and propagates a fixed-size candidate-local workspace. Each layer rereads the banks from its evolving workspace and archives compact post-interaction state, allowing history length and reasoning depth to grow independently. We compare four architectures across various history--depth configurations using evidence scaling at fixed depth, reasoning scaling at fixed 10K history, and their joint compute-efficient frontier. At 10K history, it improves NE by 0.19% over HSTU while using 69.6% fewer forward GFLOP/request. It also outperforms existing baselines on two billion-scale ranking surfaces. These results show that separating addressable evidence from propagated state makes reading more and reasoning deeper independently controllable and jointly useful.



### A Sequential Decision Framework for Evaluating LLM-Guided Search
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39231v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.IR**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.39231v1)

- **Authors**: Siqing Huo, Andrew Parry, Debasis Ganguly, Mark Smucker, Charles L. A. Clarke
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Recent systems applying retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and "deep research" iteratively generate queries, retrieve documents, and examine their contents, using the resulting information to guide subsequent search steps. When this process is carried out autonomously rather than by a human searcher, the system must make explicit decisions about which search trajectories to explore and what objective the search is intended to optimize. We introduce a simple framework for reasoning about such search policies, providing a principled approach to evaluation. The framework models the search environment as a set of ranked lists and represents behavior using two primitive actions: select, which advances judgment through an existing ranking, and spawn, which generates a new ranking through query generation or reformulation. Each action produces a document to inspect, yields information that updates the search state, and contributes a reward for a task-specific objective. Within this abstraction, a variety of existing retrieval strategies can be expressed as policies operating over a common environment, while common retrieval objectives can be represented through reward and weighting functions over the resulting search trajectory, and the resulting formulation admits a natural reinforcement learning interpretation. We illustrate the approach using high-recall information retrieval (HRIR), where the goal is to identify as many relevant documents as possible with limited judging effort, showing how the framework provides a unified way to describe and reason about strategies for both classical retrieval tasks and emerging LLM-guided search systems. Through experiments, we demonstrate that both the underlying retrieval method and the "deep research" policy employed can materially affect performance.

- **Summary**: Advertising platforms subsidize advertisers through billing discounts, and Advertiser Subsidy Allocation (ASA) chooses a subsidy level for every account to maximize total discounted revenue under a platform-wide subsidy-rate constraint. In practice, operators configure these subsidies from business rules, advertiser tiers and operating goals. Such configuration draws on rich business experience, yet as the advertiser base grows, tier-level policies can neither resolve how individual accounts respond across subsidy levels nor allocate a shared budget at the granularity of single accounts. ### PFA: Attention Can Be Progressively Focused in Long-Sequence Generative Recommendation
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39273v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.IR**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.39273v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-29 03:25:07+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-29 03:25:07+00:00
- **Authors**: Pengjie Zhou, Weiqin Yang, Haibo Xing, Hao Deng, Jinxin Hu
- **Comment**: 11 pages, 3 figures, 7 tables
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Long user behavioral sequences improve the performance of generative recommendation (GR), but computing full attention over long histories during autoregressive beam search decoding incurs substantial online inference latency. To accelerate long-sequence decoding, existing approaches typically rely on static history compression or dynamic top-k sparse attention; however, the former risks losing critical semantics, whereas the latter incurs significant overhead from repeated token selection and non-contiguous memory access. In this work, we thoroughly analyze attention patterns during GR decoding and reveal two key properties: (i) progressive attention focusing, where attention progressively focuses on a small subset of critical historical tokens; and (ii) cross-step ranking consistency, where their importance rankings remain relatively stable across subsequent decoding steps. Motivated by these observations, we propose Progressively Focused Attention (PFA), an efficient sparse decoding framework for long-sequence GR. PFA retains full attention at the initial decoding step to explore global historical preferences. It then establishes a shared importance ranking through cross-beam weighted aggregation and reorders the key-value cache of the selected tokens into a contiguous memory layout. Subsequent decoding steps reuse this ranking and geometrically reduce the token budget, preserving critical semantics while lowering computational and memory access overhead. Extensive experiments on two public benchmarks and a large-scale industrial dataset demonstrate that PFA outperforms state-of-the-art baselines. A 7-day online A/B test on a large-scale e-commerce advertising platform further confirms that PFA delivers substantial business gains while maintaining low inference latency. Our code is publicly available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/PFA-review-088D.

- **Summary**: LLM-powered recommender agents use reasoning and memory for personalization, but centralized access to user information raises privacy concerns, while isolated agents cannot learn from others' experiences. We formulate Federated Recommender Agents, a setting where local agents collaborate through natural-language knowledge while keeping interaction histories and personal memories local. We propose FedRecAgent, which enables this collaboration without updating LLM parameters. - **Authors**: Xu Huang, Jiawei Feng, Jiancan Wu

- **Summary**: Recommenders on Web platforms such as e-commerce and music streaming need their training recipes re-tuned for each dataset. Large language model (LLM) agents can now rewrite these recipes, enabling recursive self-improvement (RSI) of recommender systems. Existing agents propose one edit per round, fully retrain the recommender, and keep the edit only if its ranking quality improves. Unlike programs or patches that can be scored by execution, a recommender edit changes how the model is trained, which raises two challenges. First, ranking feedback is delayed: an edit's ranking quality is known only after full retraining, so much of the retraining budget goes to edits that are later discarded. Second, the signals available before retraining are unreliable: sampled training losses can look solved while full-ranking performance remains poor, and they are not comparable across edits that change the loss or sampler. We propose RecRSI, which generates diverse candidate edits from a diagnosis of the current model and selects one for full retraining using early ranking feedback: each candidate receives a short trial, the first steps of its full training run, scored by full-ranking NDCG@10 on held-out probe users. With two sequential recommenders on three datasets and the same number of full retrainings, RecRSI significantly outperforms existing self-evolving agents in every setting, and on both Amazon datasets reaches in its first round a ranking quality that none of them reaches in five.

- **Summary**: Multi-hop Knowledge Base Question Answering (KBQA) aims to find answer entities in the knowledge base that are multiple hops away from the entities in the question. Information retrieval-based (IR-based) methods extract a pivotal subgraph from the entire KB to locate candidate answers and then evaluate their plausibility through semantic matching with the question. However, we observed that the extracted subgraphs often include nodes that are weakly related or irrelevant to the question. Without a proper node filtering mechanism, the number of irrelevant nodes grows as the number of hops increases, leading to excessive consumption of computational resources. To address these challenges, this study introduces an efficient LLM-based subgraph retrieval method for multi-hop knowledge base question answering, M-ER. The framework leverages Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) to transform subgraph exploration into a tree-structured search process. During the MCTS selection phase, nodes that are highly relevant to the question are prioritized for inclusion in the subgraph, eliminating the need to traverse all nodes in the KB. The framework further incorporates a large language model (LLM) to refine the search direction, ensuring that exploration remains focused on nodes relevant to the question. In addition, selected nodes are quantitatively scored, and these scores are fed back into the node selection process to effectively filter out irrelevant candidates, thereby improving the quality of the subgraph. This mechanism not only narrows the search space but also enhances the overall efficiency of multi-hop KBQA. Experiments on the WebQSP benchmark demonstrate that M-ER achieves 78.88\% on the Hits@1 metric, while also improving computational efficiency. These results not only validate the effectiveness of M-ER, but also offer a viable technical path to balance performance and computational efficiency.
