### Item Representations Matter: Bridging Hard Semantic IDs and Soft Prototype Mixtures for Generative Retrieval
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05799v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.IR**, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05799v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-04 01:14:01+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-04 01:14:01+00:00
- **Authors**: Vicente Balmaseda, Dongqi Fu, Jiarui Feng, Ren chen, Hanqing Zeng, Tianxin Wei, Yinglong Xia, Tianbao Yang, Hong Li
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Modern recommender systems rely on generative retrieval techniques to predict the next item either as discrete codes or as a continuous vector. On the one hand, Semantic IDs (SIDs) give discrete models shared parameters and pretrained semantic structure, but discretizing items into hard codes discards some information from the original content embeddings. On the other hand, continuous models avoid quantization, yet typically use flat item lookup tables with no explicit parameter tying across items. We ask whether a continuous recommender can introduce shared structure without making it a discrete bottleneck. We propose CAP-Mix, a flow recommender that represents each item as a soft mixture of shared prototypes plus an item-specific vector. The assignments, prototypes, and item vectors are trained jointly, and the soft representation is retained at inference. Pretrained information is optional: SID responsibilities can serve as a prior over assignments, while a full content embedding can initialize the item-specific vector without changing the initial flat table. In matched comparisons on three datasets, CAP-Mix improves over a flat table on the two sparse Amazon datasets and yields a small gain on Steam when trained without pretrained information. Given only an offline SID, it outperforms a hard table built from the same tokenizer by 4.45-14.90% in HR@5. Given the full content embedding, it matches or improves a strong flat table while hard assignments regress on the sparse Amazon datasets. Overall, averaged across six benchmarks, CAP-Mix improves HR@5 and NDCG@5 by 6.9% and 13.6% relative to the best evaluated baseline for each dataset and metric, while delivering 1.4-161x the inference throughput of the discrete baselines in our experiments.

- **Authors**: Stan Fris, Sijie Tao, Makoto P. Kato, Maarten de Rijke
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Researchers frequently encounter difficulties re-finding scientific papers when they remember aspects of the content but cannot recall reliable identifiers, such as titles or author names. This type of re-finding task, known as tip-of-the-tongue (ToT) retrieval, is particularly challenging for scientific documents, where recollections may involve textual information and visual elements, including figures, diagrams, and plots. Existing ToT retrieval benchmarks do not address scientific search.   We introduce a multimodal benchmark for scientific ToT retrieval, comprising 108,512 open-access scientific documents across five domains and 11,499 synthetically generated queries. We characterize scientific ToT information needs through an empirical analysis of publicly available re-finding requests and use the outcomes to assess the generated queries using human annotations and automated quality control. The benchmark includes lexical, dense, late-interaction, and visually grounded retrieval baselines. We release the dataset, extracted document representations, generated memory cues, evaluation splits, and accompanying generation and retrieval pipelines under an MIT license to facilitate research on multimodal scientific paper re-finding.



### Weaver: Weaving Preference Evidence through Incremental Reasoning for Personalized Retrieval-Augmented Generation
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05900v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.IR**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05900v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-04 03:48:15+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-04 03:48:15+00:00
- **Authors**: Linyao Zheng, Zhifang Mao, Xuhang Shi
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Personalized retrieval-augmented generation requires extracting and applying user preferences from extended interaction histories. This task is challenging because preference evidence is scattered across past conversations and remains bound to specific temporal and situational contexts. Consequently, isolated fragments rarely provide complete support for a response, while preferences expressed under different conditions are easily conflated. To address these limitations, we propose Weaver, a framework that incrementally weaves memory-conditioned retrieval trajectories with compositional preference reasoning for personalized responses. Weaver operates on a Dual-Layer Retrieval and Reasoning Graph that couples an upper Intent Dependency Graph with a lower Memory-Conditioned Retrieval Trajectory Graph. Dependency Information Gap Tracking represents the information needed to answer the current request and its prerequisite dependencies in the upper graph. Memory-Conditioned Incremental Retrieval uses established premises and clues from retrieved conversations to locate missing evidence, recording the resulting retrieval trajectories in the lower graph. Scope-Aware Compositional Reasoning jointly interprets the accumulated evidence to determine which preferences apply to the request. It identifies complementary and alternative support and distinguishes conflicting claims within the same temporal and situational context. These conclusions iteratively update task premises and remaining information gaps, guiding subsequent retrieval to establish a coherent contextual basis for the final response. Experiments on personalized RAG benchmarks, particularly PersonaMem, demonstrate that Weaver outperforms leading baselines in both evidence completeness and answer quality.



### Optimal Re-Ranking Depth
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05908v1
- **DOI**: 10.1145/3805712.3809953

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05908v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-04 04:00:27+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-04 04:00:27+00:00
- **Authors**: Siqing Huo, Andrew Parry, Debasis Ganguly, Charles L. A. Clarke
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: In Proceedings of the 49th International ACM SIGIR Conference on Research and Development in Information Retrieval (SIGIR '26), Melbourne, VIC, Australia, 2026, pp. 3799-3804
- **Summary**: Second-stage neural rankers are commonly applied with a fixed re-ranking depth, assuming that retrieval effectiveness saturates as depth increases. Prior research has questioned the assumption that increasing the re-ranking depth yields linear performance gains, further suggesting that optimal re-ranking depth varies considerably from query to query. In the past, studying such phenomena was methodologically difficult given the scale of manual annotation required. With the advent of LLM-based relevance judgments we can now more easily undertake such studies, in this case to pinpoint the optimal re-ranking depth on a per-query basis. Using dense LLM-based relevance judgments over a typical re-ranking pipeline, we show that many queries exhibit a well-defined optimal re-ranking depth, beyond which effectiveness stagnates or degrades. We formulate re-ranking depth as a query-specific property and study whether it can be predicted a priori from first-stage retrieval characteristics. Through a large-scale analysis, we find that most standard query performance prediction (QPP) methods are ineffective for this task. In contrast, a predictor derived from LLM-assessed first-stage ranking quality, which we term IR-DCG@10, can reduce the average re-ranking depth by up to a factor of 3 while preserving overall effectiveness, depending on the specific first- and second-stage rankers used. Under oracle selection of optimal depths, we further show that retrieval effectiveness can improve by more than 7% while reducing the average re-ranking depth by a factor of 5 on the MSMARCO DEV collection. Given our promising preliminary findings, we would encourage the use of automatic judgments to facilitate research otherwise infeasible under manual annotation. Towards this point, we release relevance judgments, our codebase, and experimental artefacts to support reproducibility and further research.



### One Basket, Many Experts: Decorrelated Re-ranking for Financial Asset Recommendation
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05952v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.IR**, cs.CE
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05952v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-04 04:44:39+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-04 04:44:39+00:00
- **Authors**: Ryuki Unno, Keigo Sakurai, Koshi Watanabe, Keisuke Maeda, Takahiro Ogawa, Miki Haseyama
- **Comment**: Accepted to the 7th ACM International Conference on AI in Finance (ICAIF2026)
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Financial asset recommendation for retail investors is typically optimized for relevance, that is, how well a recommendation matches what each user will buy. From the service provider's point of view, the value of a recommendation also depends on portfolio performance: the risk-adjusted return the recommended assets (the basket) deliver, which drives user engagement and fee revenue. The two goals are rarely achieved jointly, because the recommendations that best match user behavior concentrate on a few popular assets whose returns are highly correlated, which caps the risk-adjusted return a provider can offer. We propose Decorrelated-Satellite Expert Re-ranking (\textbf{DSER}), which retains the most relevant recommendations from an expert-following list and replaces the rest with return-decorrelated alternatives. The number of replaced slots is a single integer~$m$ (a knob) that the provider sets, so the relevance--performance trade-off becomes an explicit, auditable choice rather than a property hidden inside a learned model. In experiments on the public FAR-Trans retail-brokerage dataset, under the primary protocol, replacing a few slots improves risk-adjusted return at a small relevance cost, and the gain survives realistic trading costs at typical retail portfolio sizes. Minimizing correlation would lower basket volatility, but in our data the least-correlated assets are more volatile and higher-return, so their volatility offsets that reduction while their return drives the gain, not lower volatility. The resulting trade-off is asymmetric, with the relevance cost consistent across market phases while the gain is phase-dependent. The provider sets $m$ to read off exactly how much relevance each operating point costs.

- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Helping users discover new interests is vital to the long-term health of industrial recommender systems, yet the usual remedy, boosting exposure of under-explored (discovery) content, often backfires in production. It rests on a production-validated insight: naive boosting fails because it injects cold, information-poor candidates that rankers systematically over-estimate, buying no discovery gain while risking engagement. We share deployment details and lessons for practitioners facing the exploration-exploitation dilemma under tight resource constraints.

- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Offline retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) on edge devices is updated through signed knowledge packs that carry text and precomputed embedding vectors. A signature proves who published a pack, not that its vectors encode its text. We study this text-vector integrity gap. A verifier that recomputes vectors and accepts them within a cosine tolerance concedes a predictable share of queries to an attacker who controls the vectors: the hijack rate equals the distribution of angular retrieval margins at the tolerance (the margin law). Margins are small in five corpora and two embedding models, so an idealised tolerance at the level of floating-point noise leaves up to 5.5% of queries exposed (at most 1.2% in any corpus once exact duplicate documents are excluded), and a threshold calibrated from our CPU INT8-FP32 comparison of a small embedding model (at the empirical 0.1% quantile of honest cosines) leaves 98-100% exposed; on a Raspberry Pi 5 running a local language model, such an attack lowers correct answers from 0.75 to 0.39. We also show that common floating-point and dynamic-INT8 inference pipelines are not bit-reproducible across batch sizes or architectures, and report pre-registered negative results on abstention, context pruning and semantic screening. We release code and data.
