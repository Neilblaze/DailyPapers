### fork() for Agents: Agent Kernel for Non-blocking LLM Orchestration
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31727v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.DC
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.31727v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-24 00:33:20+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-24 00:33:20+00:00
- **Authors**: Ankit Chadha, Pratyay Banerjee
- **Comment**: Accepted in EACL 2027, Findings
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: LLM-based agents typically execute tool calls synchronously and discard completed work upon failure. Subgraph-selective recovery, re-executing only a failed node's transitive descendants, is a well-established pattern in distributed systems (Spark, Erlang/OTP, Dryad), and recent agent runtimes (Sherlock, AgentGit, Atomix) preserve completed work through verifiers, version control, or transactional tool boundaries. We present \textbf{Agent-Kernel}, a non-blocking runtime that ports this pattern to the LLM \emph{content plane}: its primitive, \textbf{Sub-Graph Recovery (SGR)}, fires on a raw exception with no trained verifier or planner, resets a failed node's descendants while preserving completed siblings, and composes each parent's text into its children's prompts (context inheritance), all on a durable substrate with a formal state-preservation guarantee. Across 14{,}434 runs on synthetic and standard benchmarks (tau-bench, Gaia2, AutomationBench, InterruptBench), SGR reaches full recovery within budget=3 where whole-trajectory replay exhausts (100\% vs.\ 55\%), and is roughly 10$\times$ faster than an adapted Sherlock rollback that also recovers. It matches ReAct-serial accuracy within noise on linear workloads while adding recovery and parallelism (up to 12.7$\times$ speedup) where structure permits. Ablation isolates SGR: removing it collapses success to 39.4\%.



### BEARING: Who Is Talking to Whom? Grounding Acoustic Direction in Vision for Online Multi-Party Conversation
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31735v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CV**, cs.CL, cs.SD
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.31735v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-24 00:42:16+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-24 00:42:16+00:00
- **Authors**: Nader Lobandi, Lombe Chileshe, Mohammad Mehdi Hosseini, Mohammad H. Mahoor
- **Comment**: 26 pages, 15 figures, 16 tables. Includes supplementary material. Demo video as an ancillary file
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Understanding a group conversation means deciding, from video and audio, who is speaking and to whom. Leading systems treat these tasks offline on single-channel clips and align models with a self-evaluation reward. We propose BEARING, a geometry-first alternative built on one measured signal: the direction from which a microphone array beside the camera hears a sound. A label-free calibration learned from the stream registers this direction into the camera image, and an online registry of faces, voices and directions needs no enrollment. The registered direction serves as (i) a visual prompt for a vision-language model, a marker on the speaker or an arrow toward an off-screen source; (ii) one input of a reliability-weighted fusion with visual active-speaker detection, for speakers in the same direction from the array; and (iii) a verifiable reward for alignment: the named speaker must sit where the sound comes from, the confidence must match that check, and always answering "group" earns nothing. Every decision is online, with the lookahead as an explicit parameter. On 9,498 addressee-labelled utterances from three AMI sites and egocentric EasyCom, the direction prompt raises speaker accuracy from 54% to 74%; addressee accuracy moves by about two points, because telling one addressee from the group depends on the words more than on where people are. For co-located speakers on AVDIAR, visual active-speaker detection holds 95% where direction alone falls to 59%. The verifiable reward beats self-evaluation under GRPO, reward-ranked fine-tuning and DPO; shrinking the lookahead from 5 s to 0.5 s costs 4.5 DER points on AMI, and learning the registration online costs a dip in the first two minutes before the system overtakes vision alone. Code and evaluation protocols will be released.



### TACT-Bench: Testing Adaptive Conversational Threats against Tool-Use Agents
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31756v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.AI**, cs.CL, cs.CR
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.31756v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-24 01:01:49+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-24 01:01:49+00:00
- **Authors**: Ilia Hashemi Rad, Feijie Wu, Haoyu Wang, Jing Gao
- **Comment**: Code and data are available at https://github.com/IliaHashemiRad/TACTBench
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: The growing autonomy of AI agents in using external tools and making decisions has created an urgent need for comprehensive safety benchmarks. Existing benchmarks test compromised tool channels or adaptive multi-turn attacks, though they may not capture the unique capabilities of policy-governed tool-use agents, which must resist unauthorized requests without abandoning the legitimate work embedded in the same interaction. To fill this gap, we introduce TACT-Bench, a stateful benchmark built around scenario-specific stories, personas, manipulation tactics, and response-adaptive escalation. Each adversarial task targets an action explicitly prohibited by the policy provided to the assistant, while the policy remains hidden from the attacking user. TACT pairs each adversarial task with at least one benign counterpart, and its adversarial tasks retain authorized ground-truth actions when a compliant resolution remains available. We define these base tasks as TACT-Base. We additionally introduce TACT-TrustPrime, which places an independent benign task before an unrelated attack in the same conversation. We also propose Paired Selective Trustworthiness (PST), which combines explicit safe refusal with demonstrated capability on matched benign tasks, to analyze an agent's overcautiousness and practicality. Averaged across 6 different models as assistants, TACT-Base raises Attack-Induced Failure Rate (AFR) by 17.53% over four compatible attack baselines, and TACT-TrustPrime raises it by a further 17.01%. The two evaluated defense methods reduce AFR by only 8.35%--9.41% on average and do not consistently improve PST, underscoring the need for stronger policy-enforcement methods.



### SPARQL-Planned Single-Hop Traversal over a Live Knowledge Graph for Multi-Hop Question Answering
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31771v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.AI
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.31771v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-24 01:24:00+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-24 01:24:00+00:00
- **Authors**: Priyesh Vakharia, Masud Moshtaghi, Pratyay Banerjee, Nicholas Jedema, Amita Misra, Ankit Chadha
- **Comment**: Accepted in EACL 2027, Findings
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Semantic retrieval struggles with multi-hop question answering on both recall and precision: gold passages are buried among distractors that degrade the reader's answer, and retrieving them requires reasoning over the query rather than matching it. GraphRAG techniques expand semantic retrieval by connecting passages to graph nodes and performing one or more hops of expansion after initial retrieval. This improves recall, but multi-hop expansion becomes expensive and brings additional noise into the retrieved set. We break this tradeoff by \emph{interleaving} dense retrieval with knowledge-graph traversal for complex multi-hop questions. Dense retrieval locates the first relevant regions of the search space at good recall, while traversal of a pre-existing external KG (Wikidata) restores precision and improves recall by confirming retrieved passages against a reasoning path through the KG. To keep traversal both question-adaptive and reliable, we have an LLM plan it as code generation task for graph traversal in SPARQL format but \emph{parse rather than execute} the query. On full 1{,}000-question splits under a shared corpus and reader, and with a single configuration applied identically to every dataset, we reach \textbf{62.5 / 75.1} EM/F1 on HotpotQA, \textbf{73.5 / 81.0} on 2WikiMultiHopQA, and \textbf{37.1 / 50.0} on MuSiQue, ahead of reproduced HippoRAG2, T\textsuperscript{2}RAG, and IRCoT on all three benchmarks. A second, token-matched setting stays ahead of T\textsuperscript{2}RAG on HotpotQA and 2Wiki while spending roughly its per-query budget. Executing the generated SPARQL instead of parsing it costs up to 11.4 EM, mostly because raw queries return empty results, not because of syntax errors.

### SwarmLeak: Does Coordination Improve Privacy Attacks on LLM Agents?
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31791v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CR**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.31791v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-24 01:43:42+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-24 01:43:42+00:00
- **Authors**: Asma Farajidizaji, Vyas Raina, Vatsal Raina, Ivaxi Sheth
- **Comment**: 12 pages, 4 figures, 3 tables
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: LLM agents increasingly collaborate in group chats while holding tool access to confidential records, which makes the shared chat an attack surface. It remains unclear whether several colluding attackers extract such information more effectively than one. We introduce SwarmLeak, a red-teaming dataset of 24 synthetic group-chat scenarios across healthcare, law, finance, and software engineering, where attackers pose as colleagues to make a victim agent post protected fields despite an explicit confidentiality policy. With GLM 5.2 attacking five victim models through seven conversational strategies, we compare one attacker with a swarm of four sharing a private channel, matching scenarios, strategies, and a 200,000-token budget that also covers swarm coordination. Swarms show no advantage: a single attacker matches or exceeds them on every victim, and the share of runs leaking any field differs between conditions by at most 14.3 percentage points, whereas changing the victim shifts it from 0% to 99.4%. On the two most vulnerable models, nearly every run leaks under both conditions, but single attackers expose more of the record at first disclosure (85.4% vs. 68.1% of protected fields on Nemotron 3.5 Lightning). Four of the five victims leaked despite the policy. GLM 5.2 retrieved protected data in 64.3% of individual-attack runs and leaked in 0.6%: retrieval does not imply disclosure, but in high-stakes settings even one leaked field can identify a patient or client. At equal budgets, a larger red team is not automatically a stronger one; the victim model matters far more.

- **Summary**: LLM agents commonly use the full interaction history as a proxy for state, retaining all past observations and actions regardless of whether they remain relevant. - **Summary**: Subliminal learning refers to a surprising phenomenon in language models wherein a prompt given to one model (e.g., preference for cats) can be subliminally distilled to another model via training on data that is ostensibly meaningless (e.g., number sequences). - **Summary**: Cross-lingual transfer is uneven across tasks, succeeding for natural language understanding but often failing for mathematical reasoning. We argue that this asymmetry is governed by the geometry of parameter space. To test this, we use linear mode connectivity (LMC) between English and target-language adapters, evaluating on three tasks.
