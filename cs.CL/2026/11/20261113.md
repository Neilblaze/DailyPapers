### More Agents, More Samples, or More Thinking? Untangling Coordination, Ensembling, and Test-Time Compute in Multi-Agent LLMs
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17610v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.AI**, cs.CL, cs.LG, cs.MA
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.17610v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-13 00:18:25+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-13 00:18:25+00:00
- **Authors**: Seyed Mahmoud Sajjadi Mohammadabadi
- **Comment**: 43 pages, 11 figures, 13 tables. PACE Reviewer Checklist, evaluation utilities (pace CLI), and reproduction data at https://github.com/mahmoudsajjadi/pace
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Evaluations of multi-agent language models often compare communicating architectures against a single greedy pass. This practice confounds coordination with sample multiplicity and inference compute. We synthesize nine primary empirical evaluations comprising 516 unique multi-agent configurations organized along a resource vector R with six dimensions and a baseline ladder with four categories. Against baselines with a single pass (Category A), multi-agent accuracy differences are negative overall and near zero when omitting the largest contributing study. When evaluated against baselines matched on sample count (Category B) or resource budget (Category C), multi-agent systems average -0.042 in accuracy, and multilevel random effects modeling on the log odds scale remains negative across sensitivity checks. Multi-agent accuracy equals or falls below the baseline in 74% of these comparable contrasts, and study means are nonpositive in almost all evaluated studies. Across studies reporting both baselines, moving from a greedy pass to a sampled baseline shifts accuracy differences by -0.038 on average. Because communication adds no information when agents share identical evidence, positive returns require partitioned evidence, external verification, or error diversity across models. We provide a reviewer checklist and accounting utilities.



### What LLM Agents See and What Users Say
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17639v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.AI**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.17639v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-13 01:16:46+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-13 01:16:46+00:00
- **Authors**: Yue Zhao, Angela Huyue Zhang, S. Alex Yang, Hua Wei, Xiyang Hu
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Before an LLM agent cancels a reservation or deletes a folder for a user, what stops it from acting on a false assumption: what its tools show, or what the user says? In this work, we study this gap with presupposition theory from linguistics: an agent may act only if each claim behind the act (e.g., one reservation fits the name) is seen to hold, answered by the user, or disclosed while the act can be undone. A simple bound shows that when lookups cannot tell one record from two, any agent acts without asking first as often with two records as with one. We then vary whether a search lists, counts, or omits a second match, and whether the user states a claim that a record refutes (e.g., "It is empty"). Under a role-only prompt, a count of a second record leaves most of 36 earlier agents acting on the one they see, whereas listing both stops most. When the user says a record such as a folder is empty, however, a count of its one item stops agents about as often as a listing, and stating the claim mostly cuts acting when it is false. The newest Claude and GPT models always open a counted second record, then never act. When we add a same-name, same-zip customer to a retail benchmark, a lookup returning that customer first led to wrong-account writes in about a quarter of conversations, and one that checks a street the user knows kept completion near the one-customer level. Safe writes therefore need lookups that list or check competing records, and evaluations that vary what tools show.

- **Summary**: Self-supervised speech models (S3Ms) have been scaled up to thousands of languages and millions of hours of audio, yet little is known about how these models structure semantic knowledge across languages. ### Superfluid: Local AI Server for Distributed LLM Inference
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17753v1

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.17753v1)

- **Authors**: Prabod Rathnayaka, Isura Manchanayake, Srikandabala Kogul, Steve Siu, Fabian Waschkowski, Lukas Wesemann
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Running large language models (LLMs) on local devices keeps data private, removes per-token cost, cuts network latency, and keeps working without a connection. Two shifts now strain how such models are served. On one device, several agents share a locally served model with an interactive user. Across devices, a developer or small team often owns several capable machines from different vendors, which sit idle most of the day because every local server serves one runtime on one machine. We present Superfluid, an open-source LLM server built for local inference at both scales. On one device, Superfluid drives llama.cpp, MLX, or BaseRT in isolated worker processes through one scheduler that moves interactive requests ahead of agent work, computes a prompt shared by many requests once, and writes every token to a durable log before it reaches the client. Across devices, one Superfluid server turns a fleet of machines into a single API endpoint. Machines with different hardware serve copies of the same model, receive work according to their measured speed, and can join or fail under load without losing a request. For Qwen3.8-27B on an Apple M5 Pro, a chat sent while eight agents are working receives its first token in 1.5 s, against 291-612 s on llama-server, Ollama, and mlx_lm.server. On six machines from three chip vendors serving 64 simulated users for one hour, Superfluid answers all 5,041 requests while machines join one at a time and one is killed and rejoins; throughput grows from 112 to 534 tok/s and the median first-token time falls from 99 to 12 s. On twelve Mac Studio M3 Ultras it sustains 10,656 tok/s across 38,563 requests from 384 agents without a failure. Superfluid is available under the Apache-2.0 licence at https://github.com/basecompute/superfluid.



### See Every Block Once: Variance-Reduced Post-Training for Masked Diffusion Language Models
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17754v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.17754v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-13 05:10:56+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-13 05:10:56+00:00
- **Authors**: Yunzhen He, Hiroaki Yamagiwa, Hidetoshi Shimodaira
- **Comment**: AACL-IJCNLP 2026 Main
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Policy gradient methods are widely used to align large language model outputs with human preferences, but the log-likelihoods they require cannot be directly computed in Masked Diffusion Language Models (MDLMs). MDLM post-training therefore relies on a surrogate objective based on the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO), and recent work estimates a blockwise variant of this objective via Monte Carlo methods to support blockwise generation. However, existing estimation schemes randomly sample the block index, so they may evaluate the same block more than once and leave some blocks unevaluated. This introduces variance arising from block selection into the estimates. In this work, we propose a full-block estimator that evaluates every block exactly once and averages the results. We theoretically show that the proposed estimator remains unbiased while eliminating the between-group variance arising from block selection, thereby achieving lower variance than the baseline for the same sample size. In experiments on five benchmarks spanning mathematics, planning, and coding, our method achieves the highest accuracy on four benchmarks and outperforms the random block sampling baseline by an average of 12.79 points.

- **Comment**: 12 pages, 6 figures, 8 tables
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: In retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), query rewriting serves as a core interface that aligns user intent with external knowledge bases, and its effectiveness directly determines overall system performance. However, generative retrieval faces three critical challenges in real-world deployment: noisy retrieval feedback, the difficulty of jointly optimizing discrete prompt templates and continuous decoding-control parameters, and the limited generalization of static rewriting strategies under semantically complex scenarios. To address these issues, we propose RAG-FGO, a dynamic optimization framework for generative retrieval strategies. Experimental results on a diverse suite of benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed method, supporting its applicability to complex semantic retrieval tasks.
