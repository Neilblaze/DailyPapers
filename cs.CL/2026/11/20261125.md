### PeerReveal: Learning to Generalize in AI Peer Review Detection
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33555v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.AI**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.33555v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-25 00:00:48+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-25 00:00:48+00:00
- **Authors**: Zi Li, André V. Duarte, Lei Li
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Can we distinguish fully AI-generated reviews from those using AI only to rewrite human judgement? Furthermore, can we do so reliably when reviews are generated from diverse AI models and workflows across scientific domains? To this end, we introduce PeerReveal, a new detector that grounds its decisions in learned relationships between a review's claims and reference claims drawn from AI-generated reviews of the same paper, complemented by review-level textual signals. We evaluate PeerReveal on PeerShift, a new, challenging test set of 5,430 reviews spanning multiple domains, including machine learning, life sciences, and social sciences, combining variation in disciplinary review conventions with diverse AI review-production mechanisms. With regular training, PeerReveal achieves 70.52% pooled Macro-F1 on this benchmark, improving over the strongest baseline by 11.48 points. We develop an effective and practical adversarial training framework that further improves the detector's generalization using a small local generator optimized to produce challenging reviews, raising pooled Macro-F1 from 70.52% to 82.40%. These results show that robust AI-review detection benefits from richer review-level evidence and adversarial training on more challenging generation processes.



### SEEL: Towards End-to-End 3D Sign Generation with Language Models
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33588v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CV**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.33588v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-25 00:58:28+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-25 00:58:28+00:00
- **Authors**: Ronglai Zuo, Fei Ni, Junpeng Jing, Rolandos Alexandros Potamias, Jiankang Deng, Stefanos Zafeiriou
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: 3D sign language generation aims to produce signing motion from written text. Motivated by the linguistic nature of sign language, recent methods combine a motion tokenizer with a language model to predict motion tokens from text. However, both components are optimized through a two-stage pipeline with mismatched objectives: the tokenizer minimizes reconstruction error in isolation, while the generator learns from rigid one-hot reference tokens. A straightforward end-to-end extension bridges discrete token selection with straight-through estimators (STE), suffering from biased gradients and codebook collapse. To address these limitations, we propose SEEL, an end-to-end framework that aligns tokenization and generation with motion quality through two complementary mechanisms. First, to overcome rigid one-hot supervision, reconstruction-aware generation decodes candidate tokens in context and converts their 3D motion errors into soft targets, guiding the generator with physical motion quality. Second, to propagate generation feedback without STE, generation-aware tokenization passes continuous codebook expectations through the frozen generator, routing generated-motion gradients to the tokenizer to prevent codebook collapse. Combining both mechanisms enables the tokenizer and generator to co-evolve, achieving state-of-the-art performance on CSL-Daily, How2Sign, and Phoenix-2014T. Code and models will be released to facilitate future research.



### Aligned Machine: A Benchmark for Evaluating Multimodal Embedding Similarity via Human Comparative Judgements
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33594v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.AI**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.33594v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-25 01:09:44+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-25 01:09:44+00:00
- **Authors**: Jiechen Li, Hannah Groos, Brinnae Bent
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Embedding models increasingly inform how people search, recommend, and retrieve information, yet we lack credible ways to test whether these similarity judgments reflect how humans understand meaning. Existing benchmarks typically rely on static, expert-labeled pairs with coarse Likert-style annotations, limiting scalability and making it difficult to obtain consistent, high-confidence annotations. We introduce Aligned Machine, an interactive, public-facing platform that transforms broad participation into a continuously growing Human-Aligned Similarity Benchmark (HASB) for evaluating semantic alignment in embedding-based systems. Participants complete a cognitively-natural, forced-choice task, comparing two pairs of items (text or images) and selecting which pair is more similar. This comparative design is accessible to non-experts, yields clean preference data, and supports robust aggregation. Text-only models consistently achieve higher overall agreement than multimodal models, while multimodal performance varies systematically by task type: alignment is strongest for image-image comparisons, weaker for image-text comparisons, and lowest for text-text comparisons. Aligned Machine is designed to evolve over time as participation grows, supporting longitudinal analysis, domain-specific studies, bias detection, and measurement of concept drift across model versions. By combining accessible public participation with rigorous comparative evaluation, HASB provides a scalable and accountable framework for auditing semantic alignment in embedding models.

- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Large language model agents can self-improve over time by utilizing procedural memory derived from prior task trajectories, yet the distinct utilities of memories derived from local successes and failures remain underexplored. ### State Space Attention: Learning Context-Dependent Memory Retention
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33625v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.LG**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.33625v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-25 01:56:07+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-25 01:56:07+00:00
- **Authors**: Ege Çakar, Cengiz Pehlevan
- **Comment**: 36 pages, 3 figures, 37 tables. Code: https://github.com/Ege-Cakar/State-Space-Attention
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Standard attention's key-value cache grows linearly with context length. Bounding it raises the question of what a sequence model should retain for long-context reasoning. We argue this choice should be learned, and introduce State Space Attention (SSA), which learns to keep a bounded set of past representations and reads them with standard attention. A learned scorer rescores every chunk against newly arriving context and the rest of memory. A fixed-context Transformer is then the special case that always keeps the most recent tokens. We train the scorer and reader jointly, either moving gradually from a differentiable soft weighting of memories to hard retention, or with hard retention from the start. To produce signal for the scorer, we generate supervised samples during training, a technique we call recovery. We compare SSA with recurrent models, bounded-memory attention methods, and full-attention Transformers across retrieval, variable tracking, multi-hop reasoning, and language modeling. On our retrieval and reasoning benchmarks, SSA matches or comes within a few points of full attention on most tasks, far outperforms a Transformer with the same readable budget on every long-context retrieval task we evaluate, and matches or outperforms one with about 4 times that budget, while using constant inference memory. The largest remaining gap to full attention at small language model scale is on RULER. SSA makes a fixed number of tokens useful over a longer context, and since it reads memory with standard attention, we propose that it can be stacked with KV cache eviction methods. We also pretrain and post-train 400M-parameter models and measure how SSA's training and inference costs scale with context length and model size. Together, these experiments examine the capabilities and limitations of learned memory retention for long-context sequence modeling.

### Verifiable but Not Predictable: What Sentence Embeddings Know About Propositional Decomposition
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33668v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.33668v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-25 02:31:25+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-25 02:31:25+00:00
- **Authors**: Qingan Li, Tianxiao Wu, Xiling Cui
- **Comment**: 19 pages, 9 figures, 4 tables
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Quality control for proposition decomposition by large language models (LLMs) raises two asymmetric questions. After decomposition, one can verify: given a sentence and a candidate proposition, does the proposition belong to that sentence? Before decomposition, one would like to predict: how many propositions should the sentence yield? On PropSegmEnt, a corpus of English Wikipedia sentences each segmented by three human annotators, we find that the two questions live in different representational spaces. A natural language inference (NLI) judge verifies membership with 92.8% accuracy and an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.977, and its residual errors turn out to be mostly propositions that are true in document context rather than genuine entailment mistakes. Vector geometry cannot verify: cosine similarity saturates, and a sentence embedding is not a linear combination of its propositions' embeddings. For prediction, every symbolic anchor we test, namely token count, the CPIDR idea-density counter, and a Neo-Davidsonian predicate count, reduces to a length proxy once token count is controlled. A linear ridge probe on sentence embeddings is the only predictor that keeps genuine count signal: its length-partialled correlation with human counts is 0.40-0.49 with bootstrap confidence intervals (CIs) excluding zero, and it orders length-matched sentence pairs correctly 67-71% of the time against a 50% chance level, statistically matching the inter-annotator agreement ceiling. This signal survives INT8 quantization without loss, INT4 at a small cost, and Matryoshka truncation to 256 dimensions, and it works as well in a 2021 encoder as in a 2025 one. Its structured weakness is a systematic under-splitting of long sentences. We distill the protocol lessons, including a retracted pilot study, into a checklist for probing length-confounded quantities.

- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Bias audits of conversational models often discard refusals (responses such as "I can't assist with that request") before scoring, a filtering step which is rarely examined as a measurement decision even though it determines which responses an audit is capable of evaluating. This work examines what the step removes, applying seven bias-evaluation instruments spanning both deployed paradigms, three dictionary-based and four LLM-as-judge, to two intersectional datasets of 360 generated responses each. In both datasets, the filter removed a demographically skewed subset produced entirely by one of the three responder models, which refused North Korean prompts at 2.15 times the rate of American prompts on declarative content and 1.76 times on operational content. - **Summary**: The Locate-Then-Edit framework enables efficient knowledge updates in large language models without requiring full retraining. However, existing sequential methods commonly suffer from catastrophic forgetting and model collapse after only a few hundred edits. - **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33823v1

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.33823v1)

- **Authors**: Lina Berrayana, Christopher Malon, Yoichi Ishibashi, Taichi Aida, Masafumi Oyamada

- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Masked diffusion models (MDMs) have shown strong performance on structured reasoning tasks such as Sudoku, Trip Planning, and Countdown. MDMs introduce a freedom in the order in which tokens may be unmasked, and to exercise this freedom for good accuracy, early decoding strategies relied on signals of confidence or the downstream impact of decoding a token. However, simple examples seem to illustrate that resolving a token's dependencies before unmasking it should be important. For hard math problems, however, such traces can be rare under independent Monte Carlo (MC) sampling. To address this, we propose Diverse Alternative Reasoning Traces (DART), a trace-mining method that starts from a rejected trace, decomposes it into reasoning steps, and ranks them by position-weighted uncertainty. It then generates deliberately distinct alternative candidates for highly ranked steps using reference-answer guidance and a meta-thought before completing the resulting traces. On greedy-unsolved DeepMath-103K problems, DART recovers 37% more problems than MC sampling at a matched number of generated traces. Its estimated generation cost per recovered problem is nearly identical, while its cost per accepted trace is 30% lower. Post-training Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on traces mined by DART achieves the highest point estimate in five of six evaluations across MATH-500 and AIME benchmarks. These results demonstrate that revisiting rejected traces with DART can expand problem coverage and provide effective post-training data.

- **Summary**: In this paper, we prove that, unless P=NP, no polynomial-time algorithm can find a tokeniser whose objective is within any constant factor of the optimum, meaning that tokenisation is not in the APX complexity class.
