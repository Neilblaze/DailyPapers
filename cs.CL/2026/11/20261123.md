### The Temporal Residual: Fast and Effective Retrofitting of Latent Feedback into Pretrained Language Models
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29843v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29843v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-23 00:02:14+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-23 00:02:14+00:00
- **Authors**: Ziyang Cai, Xingyu Zhu, Sanjeev Arora
- **Comment**: 18 pages
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: A transformer language model computes a deep hidden state for every token but uses it only to sample that token. Existing latent-feedback designs, which feed hidden states back across tokens, are mostly built in during pretraining and gain little when added to a pretrained model. We introduce the temporal residual, a second residual stream that every sublayer reads and writes and that is carried from each token to the next. It adds 2,752 parameters to a 4.2B-parameter model and starts exactly at the pretrained network. Retrofitted onto Qwen3.5-4B-Base with 5B tokens of training, it beats a standard model trained on the same data by 7, 13 and 5 points on AIME 2024, AIME 2025 and AMC 2023, writes shorter solutions, and stays ahead at matched training compute. The separate stream matters: prior designs that feed state into the existing stream recover at most 40% of the gain when retrofitted. Validation loss can mislead: the recipe with the lowest loss gains nothing at decoding. Trained with filler tokens in place of reasoning, the model reasons in its hidden state: its GSM8K accuracy with no visible reasoning rises from 21% to 40%, and falls back when the carried state is zeroed.



### GlobalHRBench: Does Changing the Question Language Change the Legal Answer?
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29852v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29852v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-23 00:19:51+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-23 00:19:51+00:00
- **Authors**: Yuding Wang, Yuhang Yao
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Language matters when deploying LLM agents across borders, especially in law, where the governing rules must hold across working languages. Does changing the language of a legal question change answer accuracy? We introduce GlobalHRBench, pairing 240 employment-law problems across eight jurisdictions and eight languages while fixing facts, applicable law and required conclusions. We evaluate nine models on all 1,920 prompts in independent, closed-book, single-answer trials, scoring legal correctness, completeness and answer-language compliance. GPT-6.1 Sol leads at 76.2% strict passes, with a 5.8% relative best-to-worst language decline, compared with 42.2% for DeepSeek V4.1 Flash. These findings motivate multilingual training for accurate, efficient and evidence-supported answers, and deployment evaluation in the user's working language.

- **Summary**: Feedback tools and research on learner writing need transcriptions of handwritten essays that keep exactly what the student wrote: misspellings, line breaks, struck-through words and insertions. Vision-language models (VLMs) read handwriting well, but they also tend to rewrite it into correct language. We measure this on BRESSAY, 1,000 handwritten Brazilian Portuguese essays, with a benchmark that adds error preservation, invented errors, edit-markup precision and recall and line structure to the character error rate (CER) and uses paired bootstrap confidence intervals. ### SAEPO: Sparse-Autoencoder-Guided Credit Assignment for Policy Optimization
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29936v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.AI**, cs.CL, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29936v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-23 02:54:19+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-23 02:54:19+00:00
- **Authors**: Yusong Zhao, Zihao Zhao, Zirui Zhang, Tunyu Zhang, Ziyan Wang, Yi Wang, Chengzhi Mao, Hao Wang
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has demonstrated strong performance in improving large language model (LLM) reasoning, yet its credit-assignment signal remains coarse. Outcome verifiers provide scalable supervision by judging whether a completed response is correct, but they do not directly indicate which intermediate reasoning tokens contribute to success or failure. As a result, policy optimization can be inefficient, especially when training base models directly from sparse outcome feedback. In this paper, we introduce SAEPO, a Sparse AutoEncoder-guided token-level credit assignment method for outcome-supervised Policy Optimization. Rather than treating correctness only as a rollout-level (sequence-level) signal, SAEPO uses a pretrained sparse autoencoder (SAE) to build token-level semantic representations within reasoning trajectories; it scores each token based on the SAE concepts (e.g., penalizing wrong "final-answer" tokens but rewarding correct "reasoning-process" tokens), learned in an unsupervised manner, to achieve fine-grained credit assignment. SAEPO is a plug-in advantage-shaping module compatible with various policy optimization algorithms. We instantiate SAEPO with both GRPO and GSPO, covering token-ratio and sequence-ratio policy-gradient objectives, and evaluate SAEPO across three LLM backbones on six math and two code benchmarks. Empirical results show that SAEPO improves both the math and code benchmark averages over the matched baselines, with gains of up to 6.63% and 5.57%, respectively. On Qwen3-1.7B-Base, training curves also show faster early accuracy growth. Feature and token-level analyses suggest that the SAE-derived signal favors question-relevant concepts and reasoning tokens.

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29937v1)

- **Authors**: Junchi Liao, Wentao Li
- **Comment**: 28 pages, 4 figures, 28 tables
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Diffusion language models generate tokens at many positions on a shared canvas, making unfinished responses appear revisable. We find a counterexample: after the final answer is reopened, previously generated rationale can reconstruct the old decision despite newly available evidence. We isolate this effect by comparing reopening the answer alone with reopening both rationale and answer, and by exchanging rationales between paired trajectories. On a 150-question source-disjoint benchmark, reopening both regions makes decisions follow the evidence currently present--whether correct or deliberately corrupted--across Dream, LLaDA, and iLLaDA. ### Yggdrasil: Tree-Routed Recurrent Experts for State Tracking and Fast CPU Inference
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29954v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.LG**, cs.AI, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29954v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-23 03:20:12+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-23 03:20:12+00:00
- **Authors**: Cosmo Santoni
- **Comment**: 33 pages, 3 figures. Code: https://github.com/CosmoNaught/yggdrasil
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Parallel-trainable recurrences such as Mamba, linear attention, and DeltaNet offer constant-cost streaming inference, but they suffer from two limitations: 1) they struggle to track discrete state, and 2) they read every parameter at every step, tying model capacity directly to CPU latency. We introduce Yggdrasil, a recurrent architecture that decouples capacity from per-step compute by looking up each layer's transition in a large, tree-routed bank of recurrent experts. Each router addresses its bank using the current token and the state of the layer below. We prove that this cross-layer context is necessary---a single token-indexed layer of non-expanding transitions with finitely many reachable states cannot perform conditional resets---while avoiding feedback from the layer's own past preserves parallel-scan training. Because each step traverses one tree path and reads a single expert per layer, active weights stay within a few hundred kilobytes in CPU L2 cache while the full bank resides in RAM. Among the parallel-trainable models we evaluate, Yggdrasil is the only one to solve all five state-tracking tasks. At matched active parameter counts, it achieves the lowest test bits on enwik8 (1.540 bpb) and the human genome hg38 (1.567 bpb), while stepping $3.0\times$ and $3.3\times$ faster on a single CPU core ($6.7\,μ\mathrm{s}$ and $5.8\,μ\mathrm{s}$) than the fastest streaming baseline, and delivering the highest GPU throughput of all models at batch 1024. On the Genomic Benchmarks, it is the strongest causal model at matched active parameters (86.0% mean accuracy).

- **Summary**: Reinforcement learning can increase the success of automated red teaming but it also narrows the attack vector. - **Summary**: Large language models (LLMs) have transformed how humans interact with information, enabling natural-language interfaces across various domains. They are increasingly deployed for geospatial applications including map-based question answering, location-based services, and natural-language interfaces to spatial databases. - **Summary**: A language-model robot agent must decide whether to execute, question or refuse an instruction, and an unsafe execution costs far more than an unneeded question. No prior robot gate derives all its thresholds from error costs or acts on calibrated probabilities, and none was fitted in one domain and evaluated unchanged in another. We propose CATWAY, a cost-sensitive three-way decision gate: a loss matrix over four instruction conditions (clear, ambiguous, infeasible, unsafe) fixes execute, ask and refuse regions on the probability simplex, the question being the information-gathering step. Its probabilities fuse a ten-sample vote with graded scores of ambiguity, infeasibility and risk by logistic regression. Fitted on household instructions and frozen, CATWAY was tested on text-level decisions of three small models in-domain, in two new robot domains and on the external TRUST-NAV benchmark. At eleven generations per decision against one, it was significantly cheaper than a direct prompt, a cost-aware prompt and an unguarded agent in all 18 household comparisons (mean loss per item 0.28 against 0.57-0.83), in 9 of 18 in the new domains and in 10 of 18 on TRUST-NAV (Holm per set), and never significantly costlier. It executed none of the 142 unsafe test instructions. On a unanimous vote, the minimum-expected-loss rule reduces calibration to one loss-derived threshold, so for two of six model configurations a gate on the vote alone never executed a clear instruction, costing 0.49 per item on TRUST-NAV against CATWAY's 0.13 and 0.09; the fused estimate avoided this on every set.
