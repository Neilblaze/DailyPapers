### What Does the Visual Encoder Buy? Visual Information Gain in Encoder-Free Multimodal Pretraining
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26917v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CV**, cs.CL, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.26917v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-20 00:05:17+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-20 00:05:17+00:00
- **Authors**: Zhixuan Ge, Anqi Li, Wei Qiu
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Recent scaling studies ask whether vision-language models still need a visual encoder by comparing encoder-free and encoder-based models on the next-token loss of the text in image-text data. This loss can fall without any use of the image, and two runs with the same loss can differ in what they take from it. Visual information gain (VIG) is the loss increase when the image is replaced by a uniform one. It splits exactly into global information, carried by image statistics, and layout information, which shuffling the patches within the image destroys. We apply it to decoders with 158M to 1.04B parameters that read pixel patches through a linear embedding, evaluated every billion tokens up to 40B, and to 19 public checkpoints. In our decoders the image lowers the loss by 3% to 46%, depending on the image type. Global information is present after the first billion tokens. Layout information starts near zero, grows slowly with training and does not grow with model size on five of six image types, and a larger batch reaches the same loss with less of it. With a frozen SigLIP 2 encoder in front of the same decoder the loss is lower only when the arrangement of the image is intact. On shuffled images the losses with and without the encoder agree to within 0.04 nats on five of six image types, so the net advantage of the encoder in the loss is layout information, above all the ability to read text in images. Public encoder-free models at production scale (Gemma 4 12B, NEO, Mono-InternVL) do acquire layout information. On the same documents they have 0.53 to 0.76 nats of it, against 0.65 to 0.82 for recent encoder-based models and 0.04 to 0.07 for our decoders, and their released training stages show it rising. These results agree with the reported parity of the two families when both are trained from scratch and place the reported advantage of a pretrained encoder in layout information.



### ERA: Embed, Route, Adapt for Cold-Start Cross-Model Latent Retrieval
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26919v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.IR**, cs.AI, cs.CL, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.26919v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-20 00:06:04+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-20 00:06:04+00:00
- **Authors**: Venkata Chivukula, Zifeng Wang, Jinsung Yoon
- **Comment**: 20 pages, 2 figures, 11 tables
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Production retrieval systems are tied to the embedding model that indexed their corpus. Moving queries to a different encoder normally requires re-embedding the whole corpus, and at cold start there are no query logs while every document embedded by the new encoder incurs an API or compute cost. We present ERA (Embed, Route, Adapt), a system for cold-start cross-model latent retrieval. ERA spends a small anchor budget on maximally informative documents, selected from the existing target index via Cosine Farthest Point Sampling, trains a lightweight adapter with a Manifold Alignment Loss that couples directional alignment with orthogonal regularization to prevent representation collapse, and pairs the adapter with a deterministic router that escalates the rare, hard queries that define perceived retrieval quality. Across five BEIR datasets, ERA matches or outperforms point-wise L2 and embedding-conversion baselines, with relative gains of up to 35% nDCG@10 on Arguana, and on clustered corpora its anchor selection also improves prior methods under a fixed anchor budget. With 1.2 ms of adapter and router overhead on top of a ~20 ms local encoder, ERA at a 20% nominal escalation budget reduces end-to-end query latency from roughly 160 ms to 38-86 ms while concentrating escalations on the queries where the projection fails most.

- **Summary**: Large language models (LLMs) develop rich internal representations of language, but whether their individual coordinate axes carry meaning, or whether meaning is encoded only in directions in representation space, is still an open question. ### Self-similar Organization of Information in Language
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26935v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.AI**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.26935v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-20 00:46:06+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-20 00:46:06+00:00
- **Authors**: Leon Lufkin, Cutter Dawes, Kamesh Krishnamurthy
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: How much information relevant to predicting the future of a passage (i.e. predictive information) is carried by the preceding text, and how is this information distributed along that history? These questions are fundamental to understanding both the statistical structure of language and what large language models (LLMs) can learn about it. Indeed, LLMs trained to predict the next token in text must exploit this predictive information, so its organization determines how the structure of language becomes available to the model to learn. We develop a theory for the organization of predictive information based on the scaling behavior of a family of information measures, and the theory predicts that if this organization has the same form at all measured length scales -- i.e., it has a self-similar structure -- then the information measures evaluated at different lengths collapse onto a common form. We observe this scaling collapse in long text documents, showing that predictive information is present at every length scale we measure and has a self-similar structure. Self-similarity is a considerably stronger property than showing the existence of long-range dependencies, and it constrains the process generating the text. More broadly, our results give quantitative support to the empirical observation that conditioning on more distant text continues to reveal new structure in language to LLMs.
