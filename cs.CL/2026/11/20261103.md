### Swapping the Judge Changes the Ranking: A Preregistered Study of Cross-Provider Replication in LLM-Judged System Comparisons
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04090v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.AI, cs.CY
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.04090v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-03 00:01:35+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-03 00:01:35+00:00
- **Authors**: Sangdo Han, Moonsoo Kim
- **Comment**: 10 pages, 2 figures. Code and judge verdicts are provided as ancillary files
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Practitioners who compare systems with an LLM judge and have no human labels often check a conclusion by repeating it with a judge from another provider. We test how far that check can be trusted. In a preregistered study (with a disclosed post-registration reduction of the judge set), five judge models from three providers re-judged 4,000 Chatbot Arena battles (nine systems, 33 system pairs) that carry human votes, each in both response orders. Agreement with the human vote was low at the verdict level (median Cohen's kappa = 0.22), and system rankings did not survive a change of judge: Kendall's tau with the human ranking ranged from 0.17 to 0.89 (median 0.67), so the preregistered hypothesis that rankings remain stable was not supported. Conclusions replicated by two low-cost judges from different providers matched the human conclusion more often than single-judge conclusions (+0.10 precision), but the gain came from filtering out one poorly aligned judge, and replication involving that judge left a conclusion for only 35% of comparisons. Judges that agreed with humans reached a stable conclusion with no more verdicts than human votes required; the others failed to converge on 26-70% of system pairs at the sample sizes tested (up to 100 verdicts per pair). Disagreements with the human vote co-occurred across judges far more often than independence predicts, although a single human vote per battle cannot separate shared judge bias from voter noise.



### Same Rule, Different Verdicts: Rubric Language and Clause Structure in LLM Judges for Korean Support Dialogues
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04091v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.04091v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-03 00:01:56+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-03 00:01:56+00:00
- **Authors**: Sangdo Han, Moonsoo Kim
- **Comment**: 16 pages, 2 figures. Data and code are included as ancillary files
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Services that operate in Korean often write their evaluation rubrics in Korean and hand them to an LLM judge. We test whether a judge's verdict depends on the language of the rubric and on how a rubric item is phrased. We write 12 criteria for support dialogues in four clause structures (affirmative, negated, conditional, exception) such that two pairs of structures have the same meaning, and we give each item in Korean and in English. Six low-cost models from three providers, plus one reference model, judge 60 translated ESConv dialogues and 144 constructed boundary cases under every combination (51,744 requests across the seven models). The analysis plan was fixed and committed before data collection. Cross-provider agreement is lower with Korean rubrics than with English rubrics (Cohen's kappa 0.687 vs. 0.745; difference -0.058, 95% CI [-0.072,-0.045]). Disagreement concentrates in negated, conditional, and exception items (odds ratio 5.6 relative to affirmative items). A single model changes its verdict between two items with the same meaning in 11-15% of cases. Two hypotheses are not supported: models from the same provider do not agree more than models from different providers, and adding paired examples does not reduce the language gap. We measure consistency only; no human labels are used, and all materials were drafted by an LLM.



### When Strong Reasoners Join the Pool: Small Union Premiums and Costly Runaway Generations in LLM Routing
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04098v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.LG**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.04098v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-03 00:17:03+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-03 00:17:03+00:00
- **Authors**: Teng-Ruei Chen
- **Comment**: Technical report. 10 pages, 7 figures, 14 tables. Companion to arXiv:2607.03436. Data and code: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23724735
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Routing benchmarks often judge a router against a hindsight oracle that counts a query as solved whenever any model's recorded answer is correct, but under stochastic decoding that oracle mixes two different objects: the clairvoyant ceiling of committing to one model per query and the premium of calling several models and keeping any correct answer. A companion study measured this premium at 3.5 percentage points (pp) for 11 small open models on MATH-500; how large it is once current reasoning models enter the pool was unknown. We measure it with a fixed-protocol, pre-committed generation on a single NVIDIA B300: all 500 MATH-500 problems, 30 seeded samples per problem, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct and NVIDIA's Nemotron Nano 9B v2 and Nemotron Super 49B v1.5 (a NAS derivative of the same 70B) under vendor sampling, a 65,536-token cap, a pinned Math-Verify scorer, and an append-only attempt ledger, for 45,000 scored responses. The two reasoning models both reach 96.1% accuracy against 76.2% for the 70B baseline. The empirical product-coupling premium is 0.215-0.588 pp across all two- and three-model pools; the empirical plug-in selection headroom over always calling Nano is 1.27 pp; and resampling Nano three times gives practically the same empirical any-correct rate as calling all three models once. Only 31 responses reached the cap, 25 of them with exactly periodic tails, yet the capped responses account for up to 60.2% of a model's output-length-squared proxy, and long generations made a fourth planned model, Llama-3.1-8B, too slow to finish. The premium is far below the companion's 3.5 pp, but models, pool size and protocol changed together; an oracle gap should therefore be reported with the pool, the protocol, the samples per cell and the decoding budget that produced it.

### Say Less, Mean Better: Training-Free Verbosity Control for Language Models
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04107v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.04107v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-03 00:30:05+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-03 00:30:05+00:00
- **Authors**: Mahmoud Alyosify, Mirna Imbabi, Eman Elkhamisi, Rahatara Ferdousi
- **Comment**: 16 pages, 11 figures
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Large language models (LLMs) often generate responses longer than necessary, increasing inference latency and computational cost. While prompting can encourage conciseness, controlling output length without compromising response completeness remains challenging. Aggressive early stopping can reduce generation costs but risks terminating responses prematurely. We introduce MinimalLM, a training-free framework for reliable verbosity control that combines uncertainty-aware, length-dependent stop-token adjustment with structural completeness guardrails. MinimalLM separates the decision of when to stop from whether stopping is structurally appropriate, coordinating response-level instructions, decoding-time intervention, and completion checks without modifying model parameters or requiring auxiliary models. We evaluate MinimalLM across three open-source LLMs and 330 questions, producing 5,940 responses across baseline and ablation configurations. MinimalLM reduces generated tokens by 47.6%, accelerates individual responses by 19-68%, and eliminates structurally truncated outputs in the primary evaluation. Despite substantial token savings, it achieves an average quality score of 8.04/10, compared with 8.42/10 for unmodified models and 6.46/10 for length-matched truncation. Evaluations across diverse subject domains (such as healthcare, programming, law, and science) demonstrate robust efficiency gains, while ablations establish the complementary roles of decoding control and completeness constraints. Our findings highlight that efficient generation requires more than producing fewer tokens: it requires controlling when generation ends while preserving response integrity. MinimalLM offers a lightweight, training-free approach toward this goal.



### LoopKV: Loop-Aware KV Cache Compression for Serving Looped Transformers
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04137v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.LG**, cs.CL, cs.DC
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.04137v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-03 01:28:57+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-03 01:28:57+00:00
- **Authors**: Tianle Zhou, Yang Zhang, Tianyu Wu, Zhuohan Wang, William Nixon, Juncheng Yang
- **Comment**: 28 pages, 15 figures
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Looped transformers apply a shared block several times per token to improve accuracy without adding parameters. For models such as Ouro that keep a separate key-value (KV) cache per loop, cache memory limits the batch size that amortizes repeated weight reads. Uniform-bit quantization and fixed rules per loop do not allocate precision according to each layer's sensitivity in each loop. Measurements on Ouro show that quantization sensitivity varies across (loop, layer) cells, and the sum of individual cell damages approximates the overall output damage. Sharing damage also varies by cell, with later loops generally tolerating sharing better. Skipping low-attention values to save reads produces errors that compound across loops. LoopKV uses a one-time offline profile to choose 4-bit storage, 2-bit storage or reuse of the final loop's KV for each cell. The planner minimizes reads under budgets for predicted damage and storage. During decoding, each loop reads the high 2 bits of each 4-bit value and fetches the low bits only where its own attention weights require 4-bit precision. A single fused kernel in vLLM executes the plan. Serving Ouro-1.4B on one RTX PRO 6000, the memory-optimized LoopKV-M plan stores 35% less KV than uniform 4-bit quantization (KIVI-4) on the same fused kernel and CUDA graph. It sustains 1.7-2.2x KIVI-4's goodput under a latency service-level objective with a 16K-token maximum length. None of its averages on six public benchmarks falls significantly below BF16's.

- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Small students degrade on large reasoning models' (LRMs') long solutions, so recent work shortens them by interpolating an LRM with a short-answer model at ratio $λ$. We ask what student weights retain when this teacher stops thinking, comparing LoRA adapters of three students from three teacher pairs at each $λ$. Our work identifies a weight subspace tied to distilled thinking-like behavior.

- **Comment**: 17 pages, 10 figures. Code: https://github.com/LiZaiyuan0619/PACT
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Parallel decoding speeds up masked diffusion language models by writing several tokens per forward pass. Published commit rules choose these tokens by testing the predicted distributions. Under sampling the written tokens are drawn, and these tests do not see them. We find that answer quality follows the reliability of the tokens actually written. An answer's log probability splits into this reliability and the dependence within each step. We propose PACT, a training-free commit rule that draws the tokens first, certifies the set it will write and spaces the positions of each step apart. - **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.AI
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.04169v1)

- **Authors**: Yinuo Liu
- **Comment**: 15 pages, 2 figures, 5 tables. Code and trial-level data: https://github.com/yliusci/length-is-cheap
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Why do language models fail on long inputs? The common answer is length. We show that, for retrieval, length is cheap and competition is not. On five open models (0.5B to 3B parameters, four families), neutral filler costs nothing: accuracy stays at 0.97 to 1.00, up to 32K tokens on Qwen2.5-1.5B. Statements in the format of the evidence, placed at the same positions, drop it to 0.16 to 0.33. In retrieval, context rot is a competition for attention. We release the probe and all 126K trial-level measurements.



### Tokenizer Myths and Where To Find Them
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04215v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.04215v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-03 02:55:53+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-03 02:55:53+00:00
- **Authors**: Rylan Schaeffer, Sanmi Koyejo
- **Comment**: 35 pages
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Tokenization is a fundamental component of modern language models: before a model sees data, its tokenizer has already determined how the data appears. We identify and challenge three myths in the tokenization research literature. Myth #1: Bits-Per-Byte (BPB) is agnostic to the tokenizer and/or the vocabulary size: BPB is tokens per byte times bits per token, and the two factors do not cancel; using untrained models, we show that BPB prefers some tokenizers over others: the model has learned nothing, but the tokenizer--model pair has. Myth #2: lower BPB means a better model: On untrained models, BPB strongly prefers some vocabulary sizes, even though accuracy remains at chance, and on real models trained on the same data, BPB correlates weakly with downstream performance. Myth #3: different "tokenizer-agnostic" metrics yield consistent results: The "unigram-normalized loss" is not tokenizer-agnostic; its reported correlation of 0.989 with BPB comes from a confounder and falls to 0.057 once fixed; from the same data the two metrics select different vocabularies at each model size. We present a tokenizer trilemma: no metric can score only the true distribution best, give the same predictions the same score, and tie all untrained models, so choosing a metric is choosing a research question. We conclude with a caution for reading the tokenization literature and a conjecture for future work.

- **Summary**: Inoculation prompting is a method to reduce undesired behaviours such as reward hacking in frontier models.
