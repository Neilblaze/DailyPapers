### GRIPS: An Empirical Study of Pre-SQL Grounding Information for SQL Generation
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02314v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.02314v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-02 00:03:50+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-02 00:03:50+00:00
- **Authors**: Ekata Mitra, Yufei Tao, Kamalamalini Nagasundaram, Ameeta Agrawal
- **Comment**: Accepted at PACLIC 40 (2026)
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Text-to-SQL systems answer natural language questions over structured data, but can fail before SQL generation by linking question mentions to the wrong table, column, value, or relation. Even valid SQL can then return an incorrect answer. This raises a central question: What pre-SQL information helps a SQL generator, and when does additional information cease to help? We investigate this question on TempTabQA-C and BIRD. Among the audited SQL Static failures, grounding errors form the largest category on both benchmarks. We introduce GRIPS (Grounding Representation and Input Processing for SQL generation), a three-stage method that derives structured pre-SQL information to make these decisions explicit before query generation. Across GPT-5-nano and Llama-70B, GRIPS-Adaptive improves over SQL Static in 18 of 20 settings, with an average accuracy gain of 11.8 percentage points, while reducing model-call token usage by 47.8%. Our controlled analysis shows which grounding information can improve SQL generation, whereas adding more pre-SQL information does not consistently help. By separating question-input grounding from SQL generation, GRIPS makes text-to-SQL more accurate, efficient, and easier to diagnose.



### A Capitulation Direction Emerges With Scale and Is Causally Load-Bearing Where Validation Is Strong: Eight Models, Two Families, and Five Measurement Hazards
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02318v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.02318v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-02 00:09:34+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-02 00:09:34+00:00
- **Authors**: Saad Aamir, Muhammad Awais Bin Adil
- **Comment**: 20 pages, 4 figures. Code, data and pre-registration: https://github.com/saad-aamir/sycophancy-direction
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Sycophancy in language models is well documented as a behaviour but poorly localised as a mechanism. We ask whether capitulation under user pushback, the abandonment of a correct answer when a user objects, corresponds to a linear direction in the residual stream, and whether such a direction emerges with scale. Across eight instruction-tuned models spanning 0.5B to 14B parameters in two families, under a pre-registered protocol with leave-one-question-out cross-validation, within-question permutation nulls, and a positive control, we find no usable direction below 7B and a direction that clears our validation gate at 7B, 8B and 14B. Ablating that direction in Qwen2.5-7B reduces the rate at which the model reverses a correct answer by 4.8 percentage points, roughly a quarter in relative terms, while the identical intervention on a gate-failing direction and on a marginally passing one produces no effect. Probe quality, not statistical significance, predicts causal relevance. Behaviourally, pushback destroys three to five times more correct answers than it repairs at 1B and reaches parity by 7B, falsifying our own pre-registered prediction, and a sign-reversing crossover between pushback types survives an order of magnitude of scale in Qwen and holds at 8B in Llama, with marginalisation over five paraphrases per type. We additionally quantify five measurement hazards, including a numerical-precision effect that changes roughly one episode label in ten.

- **Summary**: Tool-using language-model agents often execute actions that their domain policies forbid. - **Authors**: Mahesh Reddy Pagadala
- **Comment**: 21 pages, 1 figure. Code: https://github.com/lonewolf15116/lookahead-halting
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Looped language models can stop iterating early, and recent work shows that post-hoc readouts of the loop trajectory often rival the learned exit gate of the looped model Ouro. Such comparisons reduce each method to one operating point chosen by a selection rule. We ask whether that rule decides the outcome. On a frozen Ouro-1.4B with four loops, we compared a trained stability probe ($π_1$-x) with Ouro's native gate, adapted to a per-question stopping decision, in pre-registered studies; the confirmatory study was designed after an exploratory finding on development data and evaluated once on a sealed test set of 8,517 questions. In the pre-registered confirmatory comparison, which selected operating points with a bootstrap-screened 1 pp accuracy-loss rule, the probe saved more of the four-loop budget than the native gate (+0.053 gross macro saving over three benchmarks, 95% CI [+0.049, +0.057]), mostly from CommonsenseQA, where the probe's test upper bound on accuracy loss exceeded 1 pp. Under a point-only rule, a pre-specified secondary analysis on the same test set reversed the preference (-0.022 [-0.026, -0.018]), with operating points that exceeded the budget. In pre-specified secondary comparisons, the probe showed no demonstrated advantage over an untrained KL divergence between successive loops (+0.00125 [-0.0041, +0.0065]); we emphasise this result post hoc and claim no equivalence. The probe also broke its budget on one of two out-of-distribution benchmarks (3.26 pp loss on Social IQa). Savings are estimated from stored full-depth traces, not measured inference time, and our screen carries no formal risk-control guarantee. We conclude that halting methods should be compared as signals together with selection procedures, with strong untrained baselines and out-of-distribution checks.

- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: AI assistants store values that a language model derived from a person's records under the person's own definitions, such as their important contacts. Keeping such a value correct takes two steps: detection, noticing that an input changed, and repair, producing the correct new value. Benchmarks that score only the final answer cannot tell which step failed, and they state every change as new text. Seeing this limitation, we present MemShift, a benchmark of five shifts: source revision, deletion, redefinition, writer switch, and export. - **Summary**: Whether an aligned language model refuses depends in part on which words a prompt contains, regardless of whether those words carry the request's meaning. Harm words shift the hidden state at the generation boundary along a component shared across prompts and words, which crossed differencing extracts as a single direction. In eight models, writing along this direction raises and lowers refusal monotonically with dose, ahead of random and KL-matched directions. - **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.02395v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-02 02:12:55+00:00

- **Authors**: Rikhil Amonkar, Stuti Mohan, Kevin Nguyen, Ceyhun Efe Kayan, Ronan Le Bras, Li Zhang
- **Comment**: 28 pages, 8 figures, 3 tables
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: In the LLM-as-formalizer paradigm, a large language model translates a problem into a formal program and leaves the search to a solver, gaining the solver's guarantees. Formalizers are now often reasoning models that write a long reasoning trace before the program, yet they are judged only by the program's output. We find that the trace routinely does the solver's job, assigning, testing and ruling out values until it often reaches the full answer; we call this solver-like behavior (SLB). We define an ontology of SLB over the trace, the program and its output, and use LLM judges, checked against human labels, to label 2,000 generations from three reasoning models on four constraint satisfaction datasets, each formalized in a declarative and an imperative formal language. ### When Rewards Depend on the Group, GRPO Plays a Tournament
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02404v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.LG**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.02404v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-02 02:26:46+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-02 02:26:46+00:00
- **Authors**: Junyi Gao, Yu Shi, Pingzhao Hu, Ewen M Harrison
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Rewards for diversity, coverage and distribution matching make the reward of each sampled response depend on the rest of its group, and they are now routinely optimized with GRPO and RLOO. We prove that in this setting the group-relative baseline changes the objective. The expected update equals each response's own reward gradient plus $α$ times its effect on its groupmates' rewards, with $α=-1/(K-1)$ for groups of size $K$, so GRPO trains every response to beat its groupmates in a zero-sum tournament. It cannot see a pairwise diversity bonus when K=2, sees less than half of it at any K, and often does worse than using no baseline at all. Setting $α=+1$ gives Team-GRPO, an unbiased estimator for any group-dependent reward that works with two samples per prompt and requires no extra model calls for pairwise rewards. The theory predicts training quantitatively. It locates the fixed points of a 9B policy to within 0.02 in $\ell_1$ and predicts how concentrated trained policies become with Pearson r=0.995. On GlobalOpinionQA, GRPO collapses onto the majority answer and ends further from human opinion than the untrained model, while Team-GRPO cuts the error from $0.20$ to $0.08$. On NoveltyBench, Team-GRPO avoids the diversity collapse of GRPO and significantly improves set utility.

- **Summary**: LLM planning often relies on reactive executors (e.g., ReAct) that generate a single action at each step to remain grounded in the latest observations, incurring substantial inference cost. We propose an expectation-observation consistency verification (EOCV) mechanism for batched executors, preserving grounding while improving efficiency.
