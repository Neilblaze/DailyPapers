### When Should Decision Models Compare Candidates?
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20335v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20335v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-16 00:00:56+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-16 00:00:56+00:00
- **Authors**: Fangzhi Li, Quan Shi, Wenting Yang, Yueqiao Chen, Jiayu Chang, Shuting Zhao, Zhaowei Xu, Yizhao Chen, Guanchu Wu, Han Xu
- **Comment**: 16 pages, 3 figures
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Language-model agents frequently select among candidate actions, such as tools and web elements. Should decision models score candidates independently or allow their scores to depend on other candidates? We investigate this question by comparing three approaches: Pointwise scoring, Setwise scoring, and Candidate Attention. Across five agent-decision benchmarks, Pointwise scoring remains competitive, while Setwise scoring performs better on some tool-selection and binary-decision tasks. Candidate Attention does not consistently improve upon Pointwise scoring, despite allowing cross-candidate interaction. Using two controlled synthetic tasks, we further examine whether models learn to use candidate-set information. With targeted relational training, Setwise models learn candidate dependencies and generalize to shifted inputs, while Candidate Attention shows weaker transfer. Our results show that access to other candidates does not guarantee effective comparison, and that its benefits depend on both task requirements and training.



### Trait Collapse in Text-to-Image Generation: Evaluating and Mitigating Personality Inconsistency in Visual Storytelling
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20381v1
- **DOI**: 10.18653/2026.emnlp-main.362
- **Categories**: **cs.CV**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20381v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-16 01:16:15+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-16 01:16:15+00:00
- **Authors**: Yuanbo Tong, Haoyuan Li, Jibiao Jiang, Zirui Wang, Yuchen Li, Jiamou Liu
- **Comment**: EMNLP 2026 Main Conference Paper. 26 pages, 5 figures
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: In visual storytelling, a believable character must not only look consistent across scenes but also \textit{behave} in ways that reflect their personality. Current text-to-image (T2I) models preserve visual appearance effectively, yet consistently fail to maintain personality-relevant behavior---a failure we term \textit{trait collapse}. We show that conventional metrics (CLIP Score, DINO similarity) are blind to this failure, and that abstract personality descriptors and scene-level emotion labels do not produce consistent improvements over neutral prompts across models. To systematically diagnose trait collapse, we introduce \textbf{ViStoryBench-Personality}, a story-level benchmark combining an expert-validated Visual Cues Lexicon (Fleiss' $κ= 0.757$) with a six-dimensional metric suite covering personality alignment, cross-scene stability, and behavioral coherence. Evaluating seven T2I systems, we confirm a persistent semantic gap between abstract trait labels and visual evidence, and find that visual identity consistency does not imply personality consistency. To bridge this gap, we propose \textbf{Trait-Behavior-Visual (TBV)}, a training-free prompting framework that decomposes personality traits into renderable visual constraints. TBV improves Personality Alignment Score by up to $+22.1\%$, with larger gains in models with stronger language-conditioning capacity. 



### JointQ: Joint Scale-and-Integer Quantization for Large Language Models
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20384v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.LG**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20384v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-16 01:21:50+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-16 01:21:50+00:00
- **Authors**: Keiji Kimura, Yuma Ichikawa
- **Comment**: 49 pages, 4 figures. Code: https://github.com/FujitsuResearch/OneCompression
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: GPTQ is the de facto standard solver for layer-wise post-training quantization (PTQ) of large language models, yet the solver itself has received less attention than weight preprocessing and cross-layer objectives. We target a bottleneck of GPTQ: its greedy procedure optimizes continuous group scales separately from the assignment of discrete integers. Evaluating each integer candidate with its closed-form optimal scale has been used for per-channel quantization; JointQ extends it to group-wise quantization, where a row's scales are coupled through the Hessian, and applies it to refine the GPTQ solution. Each local-search move changes one integer and recomputes its group's optimal scale in closed form, accounting for the other groups through the full Hessian; all scales are periodically re-solved jointly. The search starts from a row-wise selection among scale-refitted candidates including the GPTQ solution, so unregularized JointQ never has a larger layer-wise calibration error than GPTQ. JointQ preserves the GPTQ output format and is thus a drop-in replacement. Across four models and four settings, JointQ improves six-task average zero-shot accuracy over vanilla GPTQ in 14 of 16 comparisons and perplexity in 12 of 16. The mean accuracy gain increases from 0.0044 in W4G128 to 0.1559 in W3 per-channel, and JointQ also improves average accuracy over GPTQ with MSE-based scale search and activation ordering in all eight per-channel comparisons, though rarely in G128. An ablation shows that JointQ's diagonal Tikhonov regularization mitigates severe W3 per-channel perplexity degradation, so the largest gains are not due to the local search alone. Overall, JointQ's primary empirical benefit is downstream accuracy preservation in challenging low-bit and large-group regimes. Code is available in the OneComp library at https://github.com/FujitsuResearch/OneCompression.



### Refusal Register is Causally Steerable: Decomposing the Refusal Direction into Category-Aligned Components in Gemma-2-9B-IT

- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20405v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-16 02:07:31+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-16 02:07:31+00:00
- **Authors**: Janhavi Khindkar, Nataliia Povarova
- **Comment**: 10 pages, 7 figures. Code: https://github.com/Jank14/valueshift-icml-2026
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Prior work shows that refusal in instruction-tuned language models can be ablated by intervening on a single residual-stream direction (Arditi et al., 2024), and more recent work finds multiple refusal-mediating directions and concept cones (Wollschläger et al., 2025). We ask a different question: do refusal-related directions carry information about the register of refusal language - how the model refuses - and not only whether it refuses? In Gemma-2-9B-IT we identify two SVM-derived linear directions (B1 for harmful-content vs. neutral, B2 for self-reference vs. neutral) at layer 20 with cos(B1, B2) = 0.252 (about 75 degrees, substantially non-parallel). Adding the harmful-vs-neutral difference-of-means direction (cosine 0.68 with B1) to the residual stream of self-reference prompts at alpha = 2.0 shifts content-safety vocabulary from 2.55 to 10.37 hits per 100 tokens, while a magnitude-matched random direction produces 1.13-2.16; the model continues to refuse but switches register from meta-style ("as a language model...") to content-style ("this would be harmful"). The same register-injection effect reproduces qualitatively in Gemma-2-2B-IT at layer 12 (content vocabulary 1.72 -> 6.37 at alpha = 1.0, vs. random control 1.35), with a narrower coherent-injection window than in 9B. The two directions are not symmetric: the B2 -> harmful intervention produces a much smaller register effect, and across ablation, sufficiency, and injection B1's causal effects are consistently larger than B2's. We frame this asymmetry as part of the finding: B1 appears to encode both category detection and refusal-vocabulary information, while B2 encodes primarily category detection. We frame the contribution as identifying category-aligned components related to the Arditi-style difference-of-means direction that play distinct functional roles in refusal register.

### When the Cheapest Feasible Router Fails: Auditing Deployment Commitments in Cost-Aware LLM Routing
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20417v1

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20417v1)

- **Authors**: Minxuan Hu, Qishi Zhan, Ziheng Chen, Zhengtao Yao, Zihan Dong, Jiayu Yi, Wenxi Sun
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Cost-aware LLM routing often selects the cheapest policy meeting empirical risk and coverage constraints. Yet a router that passes an audit can violate its fixed service contract on new requests, even when candidate policies are constructed independently. We study this deployment-commitment decision with disjoint construction, audit, and test samples. Across 19 RouterBench task families, the cheapest audit-feasible router deploys in 91.82% of opportunities but violates the held-out contract in 32.22% of deployments. These results establish deployment commitment as a distinct operational decision connecting router evaluation, service reliability, and inference cost.

### Abstention Direction in Large Language Models: Mechanistic Interpretability of Abstention Across Query Ambiguity, Knowledge Limits, and Safety Alignment

- **Authors**: Koki Itai, Jun Hirako, Hiroaki Yamagiwa
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Abstention, the withholding of a response by a large language model (LLM) under uncertainty or safety risks, is essential for trustworthy models. The causes of abstention fall into three perspectives: ambiguity in the Query (Q), limits of Model Knowledge (MK), and misalignment with Human Values (HV). However, prior analyses of internal representations have focused on HV, using a refusal direction computed from harmful and harmless queries. No study has analyzed the three perspectives jointly.
