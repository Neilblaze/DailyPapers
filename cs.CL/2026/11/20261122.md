### Decoding Transformer Dynamics via Koopman Subspaces
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29113v1

- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.AI

- **Authors**: Subhradeep Kayal, Ali Dashti
- **Comment**: Accepted to EACL 2027 Findings
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: We apply Koopman operator theory and Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) to characterise how large language models (LLMs) process different input topics. Treating the layer-to-layer evolution of hidden states as a dynamical system, we fit a linear Koopman operator and take the subspace spanned by its dominant modes as a per-topic spectral fingerprint. Four findings emerge. First, the depth map is near-linear: a single linear operator predicts each next-layer state to within approx. 18% relative error (interior variance explained approx. 0.99). Second, over the 57 MMLU subjects the fingerprint is topic-discriminative, matching the MMLU taxonomy at 0.72 nearest-neighbour purity and each subject's semantic neighbourhood at 0.75 any-hit@3. Third, it is a reproducible signature (split-half self-retrieval approx. 0.97$). Finally, these findings are consistent across seven base models (Qwen2.5 0.5-7B, Llama-3 1-8B), with a suggestive trend of alignment rising with MMLU accuracy. The spectral fingerprint thus provides a probe of how transformers process different topics across depth. Code is available at https://github.com/DeepK/koopman-llm-eacl27.



### When Can a Rewiring Control Test LLM Weight Readers? C2 and an Eligibility Protocol
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29120v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.LG**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29120v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-22 00:51:08+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-22 00:51:08+00:00
- **Authors**: Vaishak Girish Kumar, Sanika Vilas Najan
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Large language models can predict the outputs of small neural networks from raw weights, but success could reflect recognition of weight-space regularities (fingerprints) rather than computation, and function-preserving controls cannot tell these apart. We propose C2, a counterfactual that permutes the columns of a network's final linear readout: every other parameter, and the readout's singular values, norms and entry multiset, are unchanged, and the function can change in an exactly recomputable way; we verify that each evaluated permutation does. C2 separates fingerprinting from computation only under three conditions, which we state as an eligibility protocol: a strategy restricted to C2-invariant summaries must already predict the target; C2 must change a property specific to the rewiring; and the reader's identity must be attested. No natural setting we tested is eligible. On tiny MLPs a frontier reader route followed the rewired function on all 24 networks, but invariant baselines were near chance and its backend could not be attested. In two public CNN zoos the conditions traded off: weight statistics predicted CIFAR-10 accuracy in-distribution, but C2's effect on accuracy was not specific to the swap, while per-image targets were specific but not predictable from invariant summaries. On a constructed population where a planted fingerprint rule works, all conditions hold and C2 separates a rule-following reader from an executor. A self-hosted Qwen3.8-27B followed the rewired function rather than the shortcut, and showed no evidence of using the weights with thinking disabled. A registered replication on fresh networks confirmed both findings (tracking minus stuck 0.875, 95% interval [0.750, 1.000]), and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B also followed the rewiring (tracking minus stuck 0.729 [0.552, 0.885]). The transforms, audits and raw records are available from the authors.



### Group-Balanced Supervised Fine-Tuning for Knowledge-Intensive Domains
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29127v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.AI
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29127v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-22 01:20:46+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-22 01:20:46+00:00
- **Authors**: Mark Heimann, Bruno Jacob
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) weights every supervised token equally, so on knowledge-dense corpora the loss budget follows token count rather than importance: on a legal QA benchmark, citation strings absorb 81% of the supervised loss weight budget while the answers being taught absorb 5%. We present Group-Balanced SFT (GB-SFT), which equalizes the loss budget across the semantic roles of tokens within a target sequence. With one tunable dial and one structural switch, GB-SFT contains standard SFT, class- and span-level balancing as corners whose geometry we characterize exactly. On benchmarks in law, medicine, code, and mathematics, we show across model families that: (i) GB- SFT injects new knowledge better than uniform SFT, pointwise token weighting, memory editing, and matched-budget data mixing; (ii) it matches the injection of masking out everything but the knowledge payload while retaining the citation recall that masking destroys (0.98 vs. 0.38); and (iii) role-aware grouping carries the gain: label-free partitions recover at most a third of it, an LLM tagger nearly all where the groups are lexically distinctive; and and (iv) the budget governs what fine-tuning breaks as well as what it learns, acting as a monotone lever on the safety-guardrail erosion SFT causes, at matched task accuracy. We release two new contamination-controlled knowledge-injection benchmarks with validated ground truth.



### VeriLogic: Reinforcement Learning over Verifiable Logical Reasoning Traces
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29147v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.LG**, cs.AI, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29147v1)
- **Published**: 2026-11-22 01:59:28+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-11-22 01:59:28+00:00
- **Authors**: ZeLiang Chen, Yunyao Zhang, Xinglang Zhang, Wenbing Li, Junqing Yu, Zikai Song
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Logical reasoning derives conclusions from premises through explicit intermediate inference steps, providing a natural basis for process-level verification and supervision. However, existing learning methods often rely on response-level outcome signals, which overlook heterogeneous correctness within the reasoning trace: a correct answer may contain invalid reasoning, while valid intermediate steps may also appear in an incorrect response. To address this issue, we introduce Reinforcement Learning on Verifiable Logical Reasoning (VeriLogic), which turns internal reasoning traces into explicit and verifiable learning objects. Rather than treating a response as an indivisible learning unit, VeriLogic decomposes logical reasoning into explicit intermediate judgments, proofs, and final decisions, and organizes them into a unified "Grounding - Optimization - Refinement" pipeline: (1) Verification-Grounded Reasoning Environment Construction grounds logical reasoning into explicit intermediate states and formally checkable proof obligations, making the internal reasoning process accessible to verification and learning; (2) Logical Structured Policy Optimization learns from verifier feedback through complementary global and local rewards, jointly optimizing the overall reasoning outcome while providing targeted optimization signals to different intermediate reasoning paths; and (3) Verifier-Guided Iterative Refinement acts on verifier feedback by leveraging proof states and error messages to iteratively diagnose and repair failed reasoning. Experiments on six logical reasoning benchmarks show consistent gains over strong baselines, with average improvements of 6.39% and 5.00% on Qwen3 and Llama-3.1. These results highlight the value of making logical reasoning traces verifiable, learnable, and refinable beyond final-answer supervision.

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29155v1)

- **Authors**: Jiayu Huang, Yichao Cai, Xinyu Li, Zhen Zhang, Javen Qinfeng Shi
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Agents built on large language models must maintain continuity across repeated interactions with users. However, long-term conversations scatter facts across turns and sessions, making it difficult to retrieve the right evidence with enough context. Memory for such conversations must therefore preserve facts with their context while keeping evidence selectively accessible. We first study how memory construction and retrieval-unit boundaries affect answerability and evidence access. Reading the entire memory bank reveals gaps that cannot be resolved by retrieval selection alone, showing that some context must be preserved during memory construction. Adjacent-turn grouping improves evidence access at matched unique-turn budgets, while wider windows do not consistently help. With Qwen3.5-27B as the backbone, ContextFact achieves state-of-the-art performance while using only $21\%$ of the evidence tokens consumed by the strongest evaluated baseline, obtaining $86.49\%$ overall judge accuracy on LoCoMo and $91.00\%$ on LongMemEval-S. Controlled ablations support context-preserving fact boundaries and source-grounded entity reconstruction, while entity readout improves multi-hop accuracy beyond adding more facts.

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.29265v1)

- **Authors**: Stephen Cheng, Zahra Sodagar, Sandeep Soni, Dinesh Manocha, Sarah Wiegreffe

- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Reasoning models deliberate over multiple answer proposals, often resulting in excessively long traces due to backtracking, even when the correct answer has already been reached (Across three 32B models, ~87% of rollouts contain 1 unique answer yet still make ~8 answer proposals on average). Given this potentially "decorative" chain of thought (CoT) reasoning, can we look to the model internals to determine if the model will backtrack or commit to an answer proposal? Operating under the hypothesis that models maintain an internal representation of their uncertainty, we seek to extract this representation and characterize its relationship with backtracking behavior at answer proposals. By querying the model's verbalized confidence following answer proposals throughout a response trace, we extract a linear direction for latent confidence that separates high and low verbalized confidence. This direction can steer verbalized confidence as expected, but it surprisingly can also steer the next token probability of backtracking, performing on par with a linear direction extracted directly from backtracking examples. Applying the confidence direction from the start of generation, where proposal positions are unknown, still shortens traces at comparable accuracy, and its effect surfaces in the linguistic confidence of proposals. Overall, our study reveals the intricate interplay between a model's internal confidence and its proclivity to backtrack or commit to an answer.

- **Summary**: Multimodal user intention analysis infers latent goals from heterogeneous behavioral observations but faces cross-modal input mismatch and output semantic errors. - **Summary**: An intervention that improves one agent can break another, and adding a stronger harness on top need not repair the damage.
