### Examining form-based linguistic productivity in people and large language models
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33433v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.33433v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-24 04:21:51+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-24 04:21:51+00:00
- **Authors**: Michael Wilson, Robert Frank
- **Comment**: Submitted to Nature Human Behaviour
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Linguistic productivity, as evidenced by the use of words in novel contexts, is one of the most striking facts about human language. However, how abstract the knowledge that enables such productivity is remains unclear. We compare people's and computational language models' linguistic productivity in contexts where successful generalization depends on form-based information. We focus on extending the use of a novel verb learned in the active voice to passive contexts. We find people extend active to passive uses in a way consistent with abstract generalizations concerning linguistic form, even when no plausible meaning can be assigned to the novel verb. In contrast, large language models succeeded only when a meaning like an existing verb's could be assigned to the novel verb. We conclude that computational models' syntactic knowledge remains tied to the distribution and meaning of specific lexical items in a way that human syntactic knowledge is not.



### Perturbation-Based Data Selection for Medical Question Answering
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33440v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**

- **Authors**: Yuexin Wu, Dayou Yu, Vasile Rus
- **Comment**: 10 pages. Preprint submitted to IEEE ICHI 2027. Extends the non-archival GenAI4Health @ NeurIPS 2026 study arXiv:2609.09684v2 with automatic RMS scaling, RSP-Drop, budget analyses, and entropy-prefilter ablations
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Selecting a compact training subset is an important step in adapting medical language models from existing question-answering banks. We investigate perturbation-based data selection using two signals derived from Gaussian perturbations of the final-block representation: prediction-change frequency and non-flipping margin contraction. After a shared entropy prefilter, Representation-Sensitivity Prioritization (RSP) combines the signals using automatic root-mean-square scaling, while RSP-Drop ranks candidates by contraction alone. Both selectors use only questions and answer options during selection. We evaluate them with MedGemma-4B on five medical QA datasets at retained-set budgets of 64, 128, and 256 examples. At the primary budget of 128, RSP and RSP-Drop obtain dataset-averaged Macro-F1 scores of 47.55 and 48.28, respectively, compared with 45.11 for entropy-pool-matched random selection. Their relative performance varies across datasets and budgets. A selected-set case study identifies contrasting clean-margin profiles among the examples exchanged by the two rules, while a full-pool ablation shows that entropy prefiltering is not uniformly beneficial. These retrospective results characterize the empirical utility and limitations of perturbation-based selection under a fixed adaptation protocol. Evaluation uses one backbone and benchmarks with documented training-family overlap; it does not establish clinical effectiveness.



### Quantifying Meaning with SCAPE-T: Semantic Complexity from Encoder-Only Transformer Attention
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33441v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.AI
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.33441v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-24 04:34:28+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-24 04:34:28+00:00
- **Authors**: Maxim Khovansky, Lev Mukhanov
- **Comment**: 12 pages; semantic complexity, complexity estimation, encoder attention, attention analysis, pairwise accuracy, readability, text simplification, transformers
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Automated semantic complexity estimation remains underexplored in NLP, despite important applications in linguistics and interpretability. This paper defines semantic complexity as the code length required to uniquely specify a text's semantic meaning, and operationalises it via SCAPE-T, an attention-derived metric based on encoder-only transformers. Existing measures either model reader effort (readability, lexical difficulty), which correlates with surface features (word length, syntax), or quantify surface features directly (type-token ratio, word frequency). SCAPE-T targets semantic integration directly by combining three submetrics: (i) inverse-weighted self-focus (token-to-self), (ii) CLS focus (token-to-CLS), and (iii) inter-head redundancy (cosine similarity across heads), using layer-weighted aggregation; data-driven learning is confined to hyperparameter tuning for calibration. Development uses philosophical prose vs. MCTest stories; evaluation is on children's stories vs. technical Wikipedia articles, with pairwise accuracy on contrasts as the primary criterion. Baselines include readability, syntactic/lexical, and LLM-derived measures. SCAPE-T substantially outperforms all tested baselines under word-length control, with a pairwise accuracy of 78.5% (95% bootstrap CI: 76.1-80.4, N=10k pairwise comparisons; +12.7 percentage points over the strongest baseline); without that control, readability heuristics overtake, consistent with their reliance on surface features. On a short-terms abstractness dataset, SCAPE-T also leads. Ablations show each submetric contributes materially. Overall, the results show SCAPE-T isolates conceptual structure from lexical/syntactic confounds better than existing methods, and generalises across two held-out domains.

- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Large language models (LLMs) are reported to be susceptible to reliability issues including hallucinations and reasoning errors. Existing quality detectors can predict unreliable outputs based on internal representations with decent accuracies. However, training these detectors often requires substantial domain-specific labeled data, which limits their applicability to new domains. We propose to address this challenge by transferring representational signatures across related domains. More specifically, we build a statistical framework that first extracts low-dimensional signatures for each domain, then uses cross-fitting to learn composition weights of domain-specific predictors. - **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.33493v1)

- **Authors**: Jiaming Yu, Xiaotian Xie, Jian Ma, Yingjie Li
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: French users may omit apostrophes and diacritics or make keyboard typos in everyday input. The effects of these common variations on language model performance and prediction reliability remain insufficiently understood. To address this gap, we construct a controlled French surface-variant benchmark covering seven task types, with 4,200 original-low-high triplets (12,600 input instances). Each high-intensity variant retains all low-intensity edits and adds further changes. We evaluate six models from two LLM families. Our benchmark provides a unified framework for jointly evaluating task accuracy and prediction reliability under common French surface variations.

- **Summary**: Combining language models is usually done by averaging weights, which works only when the models share a common starting point. We ask whether five frozen, independently trained models with different internal designs (four transformers from four organizations and one state-space model) can be related through their activations alone, with no weight merging. - **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Textual graph question answering (GraphQA) and graph-based retrieval-augmented generation (GraphRAG) have gained increasing attention as a way to ground large language models (LLMs) on structured knowledge. Experimental results across three GraphQA datasets demonstrate that TaLenT consistently improves downstream performance.
