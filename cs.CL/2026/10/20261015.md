### Learning When to Think: Utility-Guided Adaptive Reasoning for Efficient Text Retrieval
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21030v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.IR**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.21030v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-15 00:04:21+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-15 00:04:21+00:00
- **Authors**: Yingtao Luo, Kangyi Zhao, Shiyi Du, Yubo Li
- **Comment**: Accepted to EMNLP 2026 Findings
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Reasoning-conditioned embeddings can improve retrieval by progressively refining semantic constraints. However, additional reasoning may introduce over-generalized or semantically noisy abstractions that weaken retrieval specificity. Existing methods largely supervise reasoning through final retrieval outcomes, providing limited guidance over the refinement process itself. We propose AURA, a framework that directly supervises intermediate reasoning states according to how well they separate relevant documents from hard distractors. AURA further trains a lightweight utility estimator over prefix-conditioned embeddings to adapt reasoning depth at inference time. On the reasoning-intensive BRIGHT benchmark, AURA-4B achieves 34.7 average nDCG@10 while using 2.8 reasoning steps and 96 generated tokens per query. Under matched deployment conditions, adaptive stopping reduces latency from 4.6 to 0.76 seconds relative to full-trajectory reasoning. Results on MS MARCO further suggest that this specialization need not substantially degrade conventional in-domain retrieval.



### INCONVENIENT: A Benchmark to Assess LLM Generalization Capabilities from Convenience Samples
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21038v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.LG**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.21038v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-15 00:15:12+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-15 00:15:12+00:00
- **Authors**: Sara Rezaeimanesh, Chad Klochko, Kundan Thind, Mohammad Ghassemi
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Machine learning models for tabular prediction typically assume that training data are representative of the population on which they will be deployed. In practice, however, data are often collected based on convenience, availability, or cost, resulting in systematic sampling biases that can distort feature--target relationships and undermine real-world generalization. Existing tabular distribution-shift benchmarks primarily construct out-of-distribution (OOD) settings by partitioning observations across naturally occurring domains. While such partitions may induce changes in P(Y | X), the resulting shifts are neither explicitly controlled nor isolated, and the evaluation distributions themselves are often not representative of the broader target population. We introduce INCONVENIENT, a benchmark designed to study robustness to convenience-sample bias. Rather than relying on naturally occurring domain partitions, INCONVENIENT directly perturbs feature--target relationships in the training distribution while preserving the original population distribution as the deployment environment. This setting more closely reflects real-world scenarios in which models are developed from unrepresentative samples and subsequently deployed on a broader population. The distinction is particularly relevant for large language models (LLMs), whose pretraining may encode population-level knowledge that extends beyond the relationships observed in a biased training dataset. Unlike existing distribution-shift benchmarks, INCONVENIENT provides explicit control over both the direction and severity of relationship shifts, enabling a systematic assessment of robustness to convenience sampling. The benchmark comprises 200 tabular classification and regression datasets spanning diverse application domains and generates around 15,500 shifted training distributions which lead to substantial downstream degradation.

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.21046v1)

- **Comment**: 29 pages, 19 figures, 23 tables. Code: https://github.com/OpenLAIR/PremiseCheck
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Peer review is under strain from the growth of submissions, and review comments written by humans or AI can misstate what a paper says. We introduce ReviewFact, a benchmark that labels such factual errors in review comments under one definition across NLP, machine learning, and the natural sciences, and that measures cost and latency alongside accuracy. A frontier LLM checks comments well but must read the whole paper for each check, while cheap decision models, which score whether a statement holds in a text, fall far short on whole comments that mix several implicit assertions. - **Summary**: Recursive self-improvement, in which AI systems build stronger AI systems, is one promising route to superintelligence. - **Summary**: As large language models become increasingly capable of generating, and assisting with, scientific writing, there is a need for greater transparency and disclosure around the use of AI in research. However, existing detectors which rely only on text extracted from PDFs fail to benefit from the rich visual information contained in the formatting of math-heavy scientific content. ### A Jury, Not an Electorate: Label-Free Reliability Estimation for Ensembles of Language Models

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.21085v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-15 01:40:04+00:00

- **Authors**: Alessandro Rozza, Davide Mambelli, Steven Motta, Juri Taresch
- **Comment**: 35 pages, 4 figures, 13 tables, including appendices
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: An ensemble of language models is a jury, not an electorate. The answers are testimony from witnesses of unknown reliability, and the question is not what the majority said but who is credible. Most ensemble methods assign each expert a fixed reputation, often learned from labels. We ask whether it can instead be inferred from the pattern of agreement and disagreement among the experts. We model the experts as a jury: each question has a latent true answer, each expert reports it with unknown reliability, and those reliabilities are estimated by Expectation Maximisation (EM) from unlabelled answer configurations alone. On a 1560-question benchmark, the jury, with nine experts of up to 3B parameters, performs comparably to the same fusion rule with label-fitted weights, showing no statistically significant advantage from supervision. Instead, it outperforms the best single model with matched self-consistency and the strongest label-free alternative on the full benchmark.

- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21108v1

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.21108v1)

- **Authors**: Cassie Huang, Zhanwei Cao, Yeawon Lee, Nathalia Gomez, Zhuoyi Yang, Ian G. Harris, Tiffany Do, Jiayuan Mao, Li Zhang
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: LLM-based planning agents commit to plans of different lengths before interacting with a partially observable environment. Some aggressively commit to a long plan based on assumptions about unobserved states, while others conservatively plan only over what they have observed. Both behaviors emerge implicitly in pre-trained models, whether the LLM outputs a plan directly or generates a PDDL formulation for a classical solver. Aggressive commitment risks costly failures, while conservative commitment risks excessive LLM calls. This choice pits safety against efficiency, a trade-off that has been neither characterized systematically nor made controllable. Interestingly, analysis shows that LLMs treat the provided priors nearly as facts, which risks failure when priors are unreliable. We also explore hybrid strategies with tunable hyperparameters that adjust the balance between safety and efficiency.

- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: When humans collaborate with AI agents on long-horizon tasks, what happens to the agents' contributions? For solving long-horizon tasks, humans place greater importance on ongoing interaction with AI agents (e.g., revising plans) than on an agent's ability to complete the task in a single attempt. However, most agentic benchmarks have focused more on automation. Strong performance on such benchmarks does not imply effective real-world collaboration. Professional workflows offer a useful setting, as they often involve interdependent steps. As frontier agents cannot yet understand long-horizon collaboration with experts, we position Kolab as a valuable dataset for studying human-agent collaboration grounded in real professional workflows.

- **Summary**: Label-free evaluation methods, such as self-consistency filtering, majority-vote aggregation, and agreement-based reliability scoring, rely on an implicit assumption that consistency can serve as a proxy for correctness when ground-truth labels are unavailable or expensive to obtain. Using four financial QA datasets (multiple-choice and free-form), ten consistency dimensions, and two frontier model families, we measure the joint distribution of consistency and accuracy at the level of individual examples rather than in aggregate. These results reveal a substantial gap between solving well-specified tasks and delivering effective AI systems for real customers.

To enhance the robustness of visual verifiers in complex multimodal scenarios involving compositional requirements, we move beyond conventional binary verifiers that produce only True/False discrete judgments and introduce a verifier that automatically decomposes complex prompts or environmental rules into fine-grained components and assigns pointwise scores to provide a continuous and more informative critique signal.
