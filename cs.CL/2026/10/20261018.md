- **Comment**: 12 figures, 10 tables. Project Page at: https://signerx.github.io/Stable-Signer-V3
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: We present Stable Signer V3, an interactive high-resolution sign language avatar system built on 4D Deformable Primitive Splatting. It addresses three limitations of existing avatars: restricted facial expression, inaccurate identity-specific body deformation, and loss of fine appearance details. Shared Face Primitive Splatting adds transferable superquadric primitives for richer expression control; Test-Time Body Primitive Splatting learns an identity-specific virtual muscle layer for more faithful motion; and Test-Time Tunneling Super-Resolution uses TunnelingNet to recover identity-specific high-resolution details. ### What Can't Jev Decide? Probing the Limits of One-Pass Decision Models
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24929v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.AI
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.24929v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-18 00:50:57+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-18 00:50:57+00:00
- **Authors**: Wang Yang, Xinpeng Li, Yanyan Zhang, Chuang Ma, Shouren Wang, Jianzhe Wei, Liqin Ye, Xiaoran Cai, Mohsen Hariri, Vipin Chaudhary, Xiaotian Han
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Jev and its open replicas choose among the options a caller supplies with a single forward pass, fast enough to decide inside an agent loop. We ask what such a model cannot decide, and find a simple limit: Jev cannot make a decision that an LLM makes only by writing out intermediate steps. On a benchmark that varies one factor at a time, Jev finds and selects records without error up to 20k tokens and 64 options, but once the answer depends on two or more updates of one user, accuracy falls, to 53-80% at sixteen updates, because similar records interfere. Given a stale summary of the state, Jev follows it. On real decision items graded by how many thinking tokens a reference LLM needs, Jev answers 97.5% of the items that one forward pass of that LLM solves, but only 74% of the others. Jev also does not know its limit: its confidence flags the errors it makes itself but not those it inherits from a stale input, and neither an explicit nor a trained hand-off option catches the latter. The open models we evaluate share these limits. Checking the state such models rely on requires a model that reasons.

### Over-Defense in Coding Agents Begins at Silent Forks
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24949v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.LG, cs.SE
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.24949v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-18 01:56:29+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-18 01:56:29+00:00
- **Authors**: Hanjun Luo, Xuecheng Liu, Xiucheng Zhang, Zhuoning Xu, Yingbin Jin, Yankai Chen, Xue Liu, Hanan Salam
- **Comment**: 27 pages, 10 figures, 31 tables. Code: https://github.com/Astarojth/silent-forks
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Language models behind coding agents are post-trained with outcome rewards, and recent methods concentrate credit on the turns that decide the outcome. We find that defensive actions beyond what a task requires escape both. They begin at silent forks, the turns where every next action leads to the same outcome. Such actions leave the success rate unchanged, and frontier models take the disproportionate branch there in up to 91% of decisive actions under implicit budgets. The most direct fix, a checker in the trajectory reward, reaches these forks only through fully proportionate trajectories, leaving 74% of GRPO groups with identical rewards and behavior unchanged. This motivates a rethink of where learning signals belong, namely where behavior diverges, which a behavioral checker can locate and the outcome cannot. Our method, ForkDPO, contrasts a proportionate and a disproportionate action at the same prefix, at silent forks on both sides of the required behavior. On a test set of unseen repositories, it lowers a 4B model's disproportionate behavior from 70.3% to 58.9%, against 67.7% for DPO at randomly chosen turns, establishing behavioral divergence as a learning signal for how agents act beyond whether they finish. The code is publicly available at https://github.com/Astarojth/silent-forks.

- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Reinforcement learning (RL) is a promising way to improve the clinical reasoning abilities of large language models (LLMs), but current pipelines often rely on expert-labeled questions or costly synthetic-data generation. Meanwhile, the medical literature offers a wealth of high-quality unlabeled documents, including clinical case reports that trace the full clinical course from initial presentation to final treatment decisions. We study whether document-grounded self-play can convert these reports into effective RL training signals. This approach offers an effective path toward continually improving medical reasoning ability through self-play RL.



### Revealing what benchmark scores hide: Jointly measuring the breadth and reliability of LLM capabilities using accessibility profiles
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24957v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.AI**, cs.CL, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.24957v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-18 02:15:55+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-18 02:15:55+00:00
- **Authors**: Kazem Faghih, Yize Cheng, Shoumik Saha, Mobina Pournemat, Armin Gerami, Soheil Feizi
- **Comment**: 26 pages total, 10 pages main text, 18 figures. Code and data: https://github.com/kazemf78/access-eval
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Language model benchmarks assign each question a single outcome from one fixed wording, even though the same underlying request can be expressed in many equivalent ways. We argue that the quantity of interest should therefore not be correctness on one wording, but the accessibility of an inquiry, which we define as the probability that a model succeeds across meaning-preserving formulations. The distribution of accessibility across inquiries reveals a structure that accuracy and existing best-, worst-, or average-case prompt scores collapse: how broadly a model's capabilities extend across inquiries and how dependably those capabilities can be elicited. Two models can have the same average performance while one succeeds across more inquiries unreliably and the other succeeds across fewer inquiries but more consistently, leading to opposite preferences depending on whether the task allows multiple retries until one success or when repeated consistent success is required. Across six benchmarks and more than twenty models, this tradeoff is common: for about half of the model pairs with nearly equal mean accessibility, which model looks better depends on whether one success across five formulations is enough or all five must succeed, quantities we call Access@5 and Reliable@5, respectively. For some pairs the two measures diverge significantly, by up to 10 points in opposite directions. Accessibility also reveals that scaling, reasoning, and prompt optimization can redistribute capability across inquiries in ways aggregate accuracy obscures, sometimes broadening the set of inquiries a model can succeed on while making success on them less repeatable. Accessibility turns formulation sensitivity from a nuisance into a measurable property of capability, distinguishing what a model can sometimes succeed on from what users can actually depend on.

- **Summary**: Checklist rubrics applied by large language model (LLM) judges have become a standard instrument for evaluating open-ended outputs and for rewarding them during training. - **Authors**: Yanbin Zhao, Zhipeng Chen, Guangsheng Bao, Guandong Zhang, Yan Li

- **Summary**: Benchmarking large language model (LLM)-generated text detectors commonly relies on aggregate detector-level metrics such as accuracy, F1 score, and AUC. Although useful for summarizing overall performance, these metrics provide limited insight into how benchmark composition shapes detector comparisons or which texts distinguish detectors with different response profiles. We propose a measurement-based benchmarking framework for jointly characterizing benchmark heterogeneity and detector behavior using multidimensional item response theory (IRT). By treating benchmark texts as measurement items and detectors as respondents, the framework estimates text-level discrimination and difficulty-location parameters alongside multidimensional detector abilities. Overall, the proposed framework provides a principled and interpretable methodology for benchmarking heterogeneous detection technologies while jointly characterizing the measurement properties of the texts used for their evaluation.

- **Summary**: Sparse attention speeds up long-context decoding by reading few keys of the KV cache, but existing methods do not control the error of a query, and on real KV caches their errors have heavy tails. ### Beyond the Teacher: Understanding and Preserving Distillation Overshoot in Agentic On-Policy Distillation
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25034v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.LG**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.25034v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-18 05:44:26+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-18 05:44:26+00:00
- **Authors**: Jike Zhong, Ming Li, Yuxiang Lai, Xi Xiao, Sheng Zhang, Ritwick Chaudhry, Tianle Chen, Jingyu Xie, Zheda Mai, Shao-Yuan Lo, Konstantinos Psounis, Yuanyuan Lei
- **Comment**: Code: https://github.com/Jike338/SPOD
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: On-policy distillation (OPD) trains agents on their own trajectories using supervision from a stronger teacher. We uncover a counterintuitive phenomenon in multi-turn agentic tasks: a smaller, initially weaker student can substantially outperform its teacher during training, only to regress as distillation continues. We term this phenomenon distillation overshoot. Through decision- and task-level analyses, we trace overshoot to two competing effects of OPD: repairing weak student decisions and eroding successful student choices that the teacher disfavors. Early in training, repair outpaces erosion, allowing the student to combine the teacher's corrections with its own strengths and surpass the teacher. As training continues, erosion overtakes repair, causing these gains to vanish. Stage 1, Per-Tool TF-IDF Decomposition (PT2D), localizes label-predictive patterns to individual tool results while avoiding signal dilution in the concatenated tool trace. Stage 2, Token-Class Redaction, counterfactually tests whether the frozen LLM depends on the localized shortcut. We apply this audit to TCR-pMHC binding, where tool augmentation substantially outperforms a no-evidence baseline. At face value, this suggests that the LLM has learned to use scientific evidence. The audit overturns this interpretation: PT2D localizes the strongest signal to identifiers in one tool result, and redacting them reduces performance to chance. These findings show how hidden label shortcuts can masquerade as scientific reasoning or discovery, motivating source-level audits before benchmark gains are trusted.

- **Summary**: Moore's paradox \citep{moore1942} concerns sentences such as ``It is raining, but I don't believe that it is,'' which may well be true yet cannot be sensibly asserted. The absurdity lies not in the content but in the act, since to assert is to vouch for one's own belief. This makes the paradox a test of whether language models take the speech act into account when computing meaning. We present the first test of Moore's paradox in LLMs, evaluating 12 models on a curated set of Moorean sentences in varied contexts. We find that models largely recognize the infelicity of Moorean sentences, and that the judgement is not tied to a memorized form and tracks the speech act. Using the Rational Speech Act framework (RSA; \citealt{goodmanfrank2016}), we then test whether these judgements arise from a listener reasoning about a speaker. We find the models' own listeners do not reason about their own speakers, and their reasoning traces suggest that they instead recall knowledge about the paradox and apply rules therein. The models thus appear to have learned the paradox as a fact about sentences, not as a requirement on speakers.
