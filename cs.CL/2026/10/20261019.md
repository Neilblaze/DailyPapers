### Cognitive Map in Large Language Models
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25596v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.25596v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-19 00:02:30+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-19 00:02:30+00:00
- **Authors**: Albert Qin, Jonathan Cohen, Gautam Reddy
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: It has long been proposed that animals construct cognitive maps to support flexible behavior in novel environments. We tested whether large language models form similar representations using relational reasoning tasks and causal "mosaic experiments" that manipulate internal activations in open-weight language models. Models beyond the ten-billion-parameter scale successfully inferred unstated relationships between entities and generalized to synthetic entity names. Across model and task families, models generated abstract representations of relational structure and combined them additively with representations of entity identity, binding entities to roles within that structure. Models stored observed relationships as pairwise links that they composed retrospectively when queried, rather than prospectively positioning each entity in a global coordinate system. Binding was mediated by a sparse, distributed set of key--value pairs; by perturbing these pairs, we implanted "false memories" that altered model behavior in predictable ways. Overall, our findings reveal an internal organization analogous to a cognitive map that explains how language models reason with limited information.



### Text Dominance in Paralinguistic Conflict: Tracing Suppression Through Audio LLM Circuits
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25599v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.LG**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.25599v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-19 00:05:24+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-19 00:05:24+00:00
- **Authors**: Ankur Banerjee, Vishnu Guntakala, Danity Pike, Advay Roongta, Kevin Yang, Ruizhe Li
- **Comment**: 18 pages, 9 figures, 17 tables. Accepted at the InterpScience Workshop at NeurIPS 2026. Code: https://github.com/AdvayRoongta/audio-llm-mech-interp
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Audio LLMs deployed in settings such as crisis hotlines and customer support are only useful beyond speech-to-text pipelines if they use acoustic cues rather than the transcript alone. We test whether they prioritise transcript content over paralinguistic cues (emotion, speaker identity, sarcasm) and trace the mechanism when they do. Across six audio LLMs under LISTEN-style conflict conditions, the mean text-dominance rate on mismatched text+audio items is 0.78 (0.86 excluding sarcasm). In the most text-dominant screened model, probing shows speaker identity remains linearly decodable at ceiling at the language model's input, so the failure is not perceptual. Back-patching, reported to repair lexical text dominance, has no measurable effect here; but it also fails on its own source task in our replication (n=300), so we treat its null as uninformative rather than as evidence of non-transfer. Attention knockout partially restores audio-grounded output by blocking the claim from propagating through intermediate tokens during open-ended generation, whereas blocking the answer position's direct connection to the conflicting claim has no effect. The claim's influence therefore travels through an intermediate relay rather than a single severable edge. The effect appears only in free-form generation and not in closed-set multiple choice, suggesting that benchmarks relying on that format may be systematically blind to interventions of this kind. Code and data are publicly available at https://github.com/AdvayRoongta/audio-llm-mech-interp.



### Curriculum Learning Teaches LLMs Long-Horizon Implicit Reasoning, Up To A Limit
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25612v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.LG**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.25612v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-19 00:32:50+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-19 00:32:50+00:00
- **Authors**: Jimson Huang, Maxwell Nickell, Ruowang Zhang, Abulhair Saparov
- **Comment**: 31 pages, 13 figures
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: LLMs "reason" by externalizing intermediate steps as tokens through chain-of-thought (CoT), yet even frontier reasoning models hit abrupt performance cliffs on long-horizon tasks. Implicit reasoning (reasoning within a single forward pass) is useful during CoT generation, as the model can "search ahead" to decide the correct next move without explicit search. Models with weak implicit reasoning are more likely to commit to wrong paths and must attempt to recover via backtracking, while models with strong implicit reasoning identify the correct proof strategy directly, shortening the CoT. However, prior work shows LLMs struggle to reason implicitly beyond shallow depths, with no relief from scale. We investigate whether curriculum learning can extend this depth by fine-tuning on examples with progressively increasing vocabulary size and lookahead (the number of steps a model must search ahead to identify the correct next move). At matched compute, curriculum-trained models achieve lookaheads where no-curriculum training fails. Qwen3-0.6B reaches 98% accuracy on problems with lookahead up to 256 with curriculum, compared to 45% without. However, progress slows as fine-tuning continues, following a saturating exponential that suggests a maximum intrinsic lookahead to which models can learn to reason implicitly. Larger models reach higher fitted ceilings, fine-tuning pretrained models far outpaces training from scratch, and curriculum step sizes that are too large stall progression. Finally, the capability acquired on synthetic data transfers to natural language reasoning, with curriculum-trained models outperforming models trained without curriculum at matched compute on 11 natural language reasoning benchmarks.



### MapSteal: Map-based Autonomous Knowledge Stealing Attack Against Large Language Models
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25626v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.AI
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.25626v1)

- **Authors**: Zhengao Li, Chuxu Zhang, Yushun Dong
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Knowledge acquisition from black-box large language models (LLMs) offers a practical approach to adapting smaller student models. However, many existing methods rely on predefined queries or student feedback to guide acquisition, leaving autonomous expansion of knowledge targets an important challenge. We propose \textbf{MapSteal}, a student-independent framework for structured recursive knowledge acquisition. Starting from a small set of initial roots, MapSteal represents acquisition targets using structured states and recursively expands them through teacher-proposed directions. Question realization, proposal admission, and deduplication constrain the acquisition process, while root-aware scheduling organizes exploration under a fixed QA budget. These results demonstrate the feasibility of structured, student-independent recursive acquisition while highlighting the distinction between generating acquisition targets and obtaining useful training supervision. ### Consistency Training Suppresses Cue Following Without Teaching Cue Selectivity
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25650v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.AI, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.25650v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-19 01:51:24+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-19 01:51:24+00:00
- **Authors**: Prakhar Gupta, Terry Jingchen Zhang, Zhijing Jin
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Language models often change answers because of a cue, a hint such as "I believe the answer is B" that gives no valid evidence. Consistency training (CT) trains a model to answer the same with and without the cue. Four CT methods apply this idea to output tokens (BCT), residual activations (ACT), attention patterns (AttCT) and sampled-answer rates (RMCT). Prior work suggests some learn different solutions. We train all four on Gemma-2-2B-it and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct with the same data. We test predictions written in advance and inspect the models with a known circuit that carries cues to the answer. All four make the trained cue less effective, and their fixes share three properties. First, correct cues lose about as much influence as wrong ones. Second, the fix is usually large for cues before the answer options, as in training, and much smaller after them. Llama RMCT depends least on position. Third, the circuit heads still carry most remaining cue-induced errors. Making BCT targets depend on the cue's correctness mostly weakens the fix. CT thus weakens the trained cue without teaching models when a cue is right. Evaluations with only wrong cues at the trained position miss the first two properties.

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.25707v1)

- **Authors**: Hau-Sing So, Daixian Liu, Carl Hou, Yuhaohang He, Mengzhu Wang, Hai-Tao Zheng
- **Comment**: 19 pages, 14 figures, 12 tables
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Shared long-term memory lets an LLM agent serving one user inadvertently retrieve another user's credentials, but it is unclear when such exposure leads to actual harm. We introduce a three-stage audit that separates three events: a foreign credential reaches the prompt (exposure), the agent acts with it (behavior), and the other user's account changes (state). In AppWorld, a store that mixes 763 past logins from all users retrieves another user's credential for 84.7 percent of required apps, yet agents rarely act on it. A wrongly written owner largely restores misuse; trailing owner tags and added safety instructions help only some models. Agent memory should consistently record whose credential it stores, and evaluations should measure behavior, not only exposure.

- **Summary**: Sycophancy, where an LLM becomes overly agreeable, deferential, or flattering toward the user, has been identified as a major failure mode. - **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.25735v1)

- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Before language models are released, they are tested for harmful traits such as willingness to carry out dangerous requests, deception, power seeking, and sycophancy. The safe behavior these tests certify may degrade as models are modified, by topical fine-tuning, new training data, or changes to settings such as the depth of their reasoning. Most work reports such drift as single summary rates scored by another AI model, without the error analysis needed to interpret them. These results demonstrate the importance of testing safety with reasoning turned both on and off.

- **Summary**: Reflection lets LLM agents improve from their own attempts, but the memory it builds is local to a single task, so an agent reflecting on its own tends to revisit the same explanation for failure. - **Summary**: LLM agents can improve across tasks without updating model parameters by storing experience from their own executions in external memory and reusing it to guide later decisions.
