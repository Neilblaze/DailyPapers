### Calculation and rejection diverge in language models tested at known mechanical limits
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38096v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CE**, cs.AI, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.38096v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-28 00:28:46+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-28 00:28:46+00:00
- **Authors**: Shaoliang Yang, Jun Wang
- **Comment**: 94 pages, including Supplementary Information. Data and code: https://github.com/nbbllxx0/Certified-feasibility-margins
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: When a language model returns engineering quantities with the status "solved", software that trusts it passes them on, even when the requested design is physically impossible. Such failures could reflect poor calculation, how the answer is requested, or a failure to judge feasibility. We set beam loads and disk speeds at known percentages above or below plastic collapse or burst, measured each model's limit calculation separately and tested 12 models in 4,860 calls, including follow-up experiments on new designs. We find that a model can recognize an impossible design when asked only whether it is feasible, yet accept the same design, reporting "solved", when asked for a full answer. Three models accepted beams 10 to 30% beyond collapse despite precise limit calculations in separate questions, and many accepted replies stated the violation. Asked only about feasibility, Opus 5 and Gemini 3.8 Flash rejected every impossible new design they were given, including those accepted in full answers, without false alarms. Rewording when to decline or changing the answer format raised rejection on the original beams, but not consistently on new designs. Software and benchmarks using a model's status should test feasibility judgment and full-answer rejection separately, and report the exact prompt.



### ATI-OPD: Adaptive Teacher Intervention for Stable Long-Sequence On-Policy Distillation
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38160v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.38160v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-28 02:00:26+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-28 02:00:26+00:00
- **Authors**: Jiannan Guan, Youxin Jiang, Yutai Hou, Qiguang Chen, Yirong Zeng, Haitao Wang, Dengyun Peng, Zheng Yan, Jinhao Liu, Tao Ye, Dandan Tu, Wanxiang Che
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: On-policy distillation (OPD) trains a student on its own responses using token-level feedback from a teacher. As responses grow longer, the student's prefix can depart from the teacher's own reasoning, weakening its feedback on later tokens. Updates based on this feedback can reinforce the same deviations in later responses and destabilize training. Prior approaches such as length control and loss reweighting limit learning from problematic responses but leave their prefixes unchanged. Fixed cutoffs can also discard later reasoning. We propose \textbf{Adaptive Teacher Intervention for On-Policy Distillation (ATI-OPD)} to improve subsequent teacher guidance by revising the student's reasoning prefix. ATI-OPD replaces the earliest high-disagreement segment with a short teacher segment, then lets the student resume its own reasoning from the revised text. To handle renewed disagreement, it excludes persistently divergent suffixes from the distillation loss while retaining useful student continuation. Training diagnostics show less teacher-student disagreement, fewer gradient spikes, and fewer extremely long responses. Across five mathematical reasoning benchmarks and two teacher-student pairs, ATI-OPD improves average student-only accuracy over standard OPD.

- **Summary**: We present EasyProver, a Lean proving system built from recursive agents with isolated contexts, asynchronous search, parallel tactic probing, and an elastic elaboration service. - **Summary**: Language models increasingly create 3D content through code, with practical workflows often relying on editing existing programs rather than regenerating them, for speed and precise control. - **Summary**: Automatic judges offer inexpensive information about model outputs, but an evaluation team must decide how to turn that information into fewer human annotations. - **Summary**: As large language models (LLMs) reach users worldwide, personal counseling has emerged as one of their most consequential applications, sometimes in life-critical situations. Yet broader access does not mean equal support. The text these models learn from disproportionately reflects people with the freedom to act, embedding an implicit assumption that users can leave, report, seek help, or appeal. Existing benchmarks largely inherit this assumption, overlooking users whose agency is constrained by structural conditions such as war, statelessness, and displacement. Meanwhile, models are increasingly shaped by normative frameworks such as Constitutional AI, often grounded in ideals like human rights, even as many users live where those rights offer little protection or recourse.   We introduce HEARSAYBENCH, a human-authored dataset of 400 test cases with in-region human verification grounded in scenarios where structural barriers stand between a person and the exercise of their rights. Built from United Nations and other human rights reports, the scenarios span over 80 regions and cover social, environmental, and personal constraints. Drawing on Nobel laureate Amartya Sen's Capabilities Approach, HEARSAYBENCH tests whether models can distinguish what a person is formally entitled to do from what they are actually able to do. Across 14 proprietary and open-weight models, every model infers a user's situation more accurately than it advises on it. Stronger models narrow this gap, but their recommendations remain unsafe to follow. Models may understand lives shaped by constraint, yet that knowledge remains hearsay if it cannot translate into advice people can act on. Those people are not alignment's long tail; they are among the users for whom good guidance matters most. HEARSAYBENCH makes this failure measurable, a step toward systems that serve the world as it is rather than as assumed.
