### Governed Self-Evolution for Autonomous Investing: A Multi-Agent LLM System with an Invariant Core

- **DOI**: None

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.30557v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-22 00:22:47+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-22 00:22:47+00:00
- **Authors**: Yebo Feng, Jingyang Dai, Liangrui Pan, Nelson Lau, Jiahua Xu, Yang Liu
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: LLM agents that act on live Web data must combine heterogeneous evidence, remember what they have done and improve from outcomes without weakening their own safeguards. We study this problem in autonomous investing, where every action is irreversible and based on untrusted Web content. We present Steward, a multi-agent LLM system that draws the boundary of self-evolution in its type system. First, nine role-specialised agents decide jointly: four analysts read news feeds, SEC filings, macroeconomic series and market data and emit typed reports, from which a Portfolio Manager sets target positions that a protected Risk Manager audits. Second, a seven-layer shared memory, assembled by code from the system's own record, gives each decision its position context, the market narrative, calibration and the agent's past decisions. Third, the system improves itself by review: code grades every trade and forecast nightly and writes the grades into memory, and each quarter a reflector proposes prompt learnings that a deterministic gate applies reversibly and replay of recorded inputs verifies. All three operate around an invariant core of order arithmetic, risk rules and discipline-agent prompts that no self-edit can reach. In a five-month live deployment (102 trading days, 1,632 LLM calls), the system contained a 24.6% sector drawdown to a 0.3% loss; in replay of its recorded inputs, a single model given the same raw data traded more and lost 1.35 points against the market over five days where the joint design showed no significant loss, and no injected instruction was followed; and a 2015-2026 backtest shows the core alone lowering the maximum drawdown under all 50 random entry policies. The reflector traced the dominant failure to its root cause, and the gate applied a learning that the agent adopted on trigger evenings and on no control evening. ### TT-SAX: A Two-Track Time Series Symbolization for Natural Language Shape Querying
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30593v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.DB**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.30593v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-22 01:49:10+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-22 01:49:10+00:00
- **Authors**: Zhao Tan, Xianglong Bao, Zhipeng Xi, Ming Jin
- **Comment**: 14 pages, 8 figures, 2 tables. Accepted at the Australasian Database Conference (ADC 2026). This is the submitted manuscript prior to peer review
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Natural language querying for time series databases aims to let users find what they need in long histories of data by asking in plain language. Shape queries ask how a series moves. They are the main challenge of this task. Recent work makes them feasible by using symbolization as a bridge between the query and the long history. However, existing symbolizations were designed for compression, similarity indexing, or classification. What a symbolization should provide for natural language querying has not been systematically studied. We therefore examine the definitions of six classical symbolizations against this task and identify three limitations. (1) Direction is missing from the string, (2) direction is measured at too small a scale, and (3) the letters are arbitrary. Three design principles follow. We follow these principles and make a minimal redesign of SAX called TT-SAX (two-track SAX). The level letters stay. Each segment gains a trend letter. It is measured across neighboring segments rather than inside one, and graded to match the adjectives that queries use. We conduct extensive experiments on the public NLQTSBench benchmark, comparing TT-SAX with the six classical symbolizations across six LLMs. TT-SAX outperforms all six on shape queries, and ablations trace its gains back to the three limitations one by one. This paper is the first systematic study of time series symbolization with natural language querying as the design goal. It provides a foundation for future research on this task. Our code is available at https://github.com/Atlamtiz/tt-sax.



### The Fusion Tax: A Diagnostic Analysis of When Hybrid Retrieval Hurts
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30612v1
- **DOI**: 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3743502
- **Categories**: **cs.IR**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.30612v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-22 02:24:08+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-22 02:24:08+00:00
- **Authors**: Aaditya Chauhan, Arun Agarwal
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: IEEE Access, vol. 14, pp. 159620-159637, 2026
- **Summary**: Hybrid retrieval, which fuses a sparse lexical retriever with a dense neural one, is widely recommended in the software documentation surveyed in September 2026, usually with reciprocal rank fusion (RRF), on the strength of aggregate gains. We ask what fusion costs on individual queries, treating "do not fuse" as a first-class outcome, in the setting in which hybrid retrieval is deployed: a standard Anserini BM25, five dense encoders spanning 22M-596M parameters and three model generations, a learned-sparse (SPLADE) alternative, and a cross-encoder second stage, on seven BEIR datasets and three fusion functions. Even the best method, dense-weighted score interpolation, ranks relevant documents below the better of its two components on 13-48% of queries and below the deployable dense-only retriever on 8-21% (14-48% and 11-25% at a weight selected under leave-one-corpus-out validation), while beating dense-only in aggregate on every corpus with the primary encoder; the downside is significant under loss-averse risk-sensitive evaluation on six of the seven corpora. We call this loss the fusion tax. It is mostly a ranking effect that preserves recall in aggregate, it is most likely on the queries that only one retriever covers, and it persists across the encoder ladder and the SPLADE substitution. RRF carries the higher tax wherever the dense retriever is clearly stronger and only beats dense-only where the two components are balanced. Fusion failure is weakly predictable within a corpus but transfers unreliably across corpora, because the features that predict it change sign with the sparse-dense balance. A cross-encoder recovers part of the tax only where it is itself reliable. We distill these findings into guidance that keys the fusion decision to the measured sparse-dense balance of the target corpus.



### What the Specification Did Not Say: Specification-Oracle Gaps in Test-Graded Coding-Agent Benchmarks

- **Authors**: Fei Ding
- **Comment**: 13 pages

- **Summary**: Coding-agent benchmarks often describe a task in natural language but grade it with tests taken from the upstream project. Passing those tests can require information the description never states: a constant's name, a module path, an exact message. We call this a specification-oracle gap: where it exists, a benchmark also measures whether an agent reproduces upstream implementation details. We formalise the gap, including the inconsistencies that chains of dependent tasks add, and build a static audit for five languages that classifies every name-, path- and literal-level dependency of every scored test (held-out precision 89.2%, recall 92.9%). Across four further benchmarks, prevalence appears to follow construction: a third or more of the tasks are affected where specifications are release notes or LLM summaries, almost none where tasks are screened or written for their tests; how much a gap costs depends on how the harness grades. We release the audit, corrected filter lists and specification addenda.

- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Large language models increasingly act as controllers in structured decision pipelines, yet their decisions are still executed by autoregressive text decoding. - **Summary**: Singular-value spectra encode important structure in transformer feed-forward projections, yet their causal role in downstream behavior remains underexplored. ### Scaling Laws for Safety Probes
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30678v2
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.AI**, cs.CL, cs.CR, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.30678v2)
- **Published**: 2026-10-22 03:43:33+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-23 01:55:37+00:00
- **Authors**: Yuchen Wu, Jerry Wei
- **Comment**: 62 pages, 23 figures, 33 tables
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Safety probes trained on a model's activations are a standard tool for monitoring language models in real time. Each time a new model is released or a new harm area needs to be monitored, the probe must be retrained, which requires new labels and a choice of probe architecture. Yet neither the model nor the labels tell us in advance how well the resulting probe will perform. Finding out requires training and comparing many probe configurations. In this paper, we ask whether probe performance can instead be predicted in advance from model size and the number of labels. Existing safety datasets are insufficient for this, as they are either limited in scale or already saturated. We therefore construct six new safety datasets of sufficient size and difficulty. On these datasets, we train two probe architectures on 20 base models from five model families, with 32 to $10^{6}$ labels for each model. We measure performance using probe error, defined as 1 - AUROC, where lower values are better. We find that probe error follows two simple scaling laws. First, the lowest probe error a model can reach falls as a power law in its size. Second, probe error decreases towards its floor as the number of examples increases; the slope increases as the target model size increases. Both laws hold across tasks, model families and probe architectures, and only their fitted constants differ. When fitted on the smallest models of a family, the laws predict the error of the next larger model to within 0.02 in 72% of cases. They also identify whether more labels or a larger model is the cheaper way to reach a target error in 73% of cases. Finally, they choose the probe architecture with an extra error of only 0.006 on average when they pick the worse one. These laws allow safety researchers to determine how to create the best probe for any given model and label budget.

- **Summary**: This paper studies the local geometry of semantic meaning in large language models.
