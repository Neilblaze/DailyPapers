### Everyone Was Right: Lexical Representations in Language Models Are Both Abstract and Holistic
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17538v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.17538v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-13 01:04:05+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-13 01:04:05+00:00
- **Authors**: Zachary Nicholas Houghton, Yu Zhou, Dan Pluth, Vijay K. Gurbani, Vsevolod Kapatsinski
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Lexical representations can encode both abstract information and information specific to particular contexts. We investigate how these forms of information are organized in language models by decomposing contextual word representations into item, class, and word-class interaction components. Across six pretrained transformers and two linguistic categories, we find that the interaction is consistently present, grows across layers, and contains information associated with both item and class. Unlike the nearly orthogonal item and class effects, the interaction overlaps with both and encodes class information beyond the shared class effect. An interaction also emerges in artificial-language learners even when the input contains only additive structure. Together, these findings show that lexical representations combine abstract information with structure specific to particular word-class combinations.



### Many Steps into One: Reasoning Contraction and the Limits of Safe Memory Reuse for LLM Agents
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17544v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.AI
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.17544v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-13 01:16:37+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-13 01:16:37+00:00
- **Authors**: Jianghong Zhou, Hao Rong, Chen Lin, Daoan Zhang
- **Comment**: 57 pages
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Thought templates, skill libraries, agent memories and caches turn many reasoning steps into one. We call this reasoning contraction. A contracted memory saves reasoning steps and errors until its facts change, then quietly answers wrong. Most systems trust memories by counting successes and failures. We prove that such counters are badly tuned change detectors: a symmetric counter notices a partial change only after a power of its false-alarm horizon, and an unclipped one may never notice it. On five LLMs answering multi-hop questions over edited facts, ExpeL- and ACE-style counters serve 7.0-13.5x more stale answers than certified memories given the same information, and on code tasks with an exact verifier 35-73x more. Safety also has a price. When facts change and each memory is judged only by its own verified outcomes, one number, the certification load, sets how much reasoning any safe memory can skip, and under Zipf demand it yields a scaling law. Our recipe weighs each memory's failures by its own likelihood ratio, certifies its utility against live reasoning, and restarts the certificate when facts change, with the likelihood-ratio detector as the safety net for unannounced changes; for large edits it also invalidates the memory.

### Dissociating Answerability from Extraction in LLM Question Answering over Radiology Reports
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17576v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.AI, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.17576v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-13 01:55:59+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-13 01:55:59+00:00
- **Authors**: Priyanshu Sinha, Kelsey McDonald
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Answering questions from radiology reports involves two decisions: whether the report contains an answer and which span gives it. A false answer presents an unsupported claim as fact; an over-abstention hides what the report says. Yet RadQA results are reported as a single F1 that merges both. We propose an evaluation method that separates them, reporting false-answer and over-abstention rates beside F1 and splitting F1 changes into extraction and answerability terms. We apply it to 13 LLMs on the RadQA test set, zero-shot and with three cumulative demonstration sets, the last adding an unanswerable example. Models with similar F1 differed more than twofold in false-answer rate. The unanswerable example changed when models answered, not how well they extracted: 12 of 13 models abstained more often, and three-quarters of the answers they withdrew had F1 $\geq$ 0.5. Within models, false answers showed no clear association with extraction quality, but acting on the decision itself helped: keeping an answer only when enough other models also answered lowered the zero-shot false-answer rate by 4 points without a detectable F1 change. Because the two decisions can change independently, we suggest radiology QA evaluations report them separately, not through F1 alone.

### Facts Don't Speak Louder than Words: The Behavioral Essence of Long-CoT Distillation
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17607v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.17607v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-13 02:29:15+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-13 02:29:15+00:00
- **Authors**: Yongcan Yu, Jian Liang, Lingxiao He, Kuangpu Guo, Yanbo Wang, Shuo Lu, Meng Wang, Ran He
- **Comment**: Accepted to NeurIPS 2026
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: While long chain-of-thought (CoT) distillation significantly improves the reasoning abilities of language models, its underlying mechanism remains poorly understood. Current pipelines generally rely on rejection sampling to gather reasoning traces across diverse prompts, assuming that factual correctness and broad problem coverage are indispensable. Surprisingly, we find that training exclusively on flawed reasoning trajectories or a restricted prompt set yields nearly comparable performance. Driven by this counterintuitive finding, we propose the Behavioral Hypothesis: the essence of Long-CoT distillation is behavioral alignment with structured reasoning patterns, rather than factual knowledge memorization. We then validate this by demonstrating substantial reasoning gains even when models are fine-tuned exclusively on previously mastered problems-effectively isolating behavioral alignment from novel knowledge injection. Having established that reasoning patterns are the true bottleneck, we investigate how to learn them across varying levels of complexity optimally. We reveal a critical interplay: while the recent probability-based loss excels at learning simple patterns, the standard cross-entropy loss is essential for scaling to complex CoTs. We believe these insights demystify CoT distillation and provide a principled foundation for the future development of reasoning models. Code is available on https://github.com/yuyongcan/Long-CoT-Distillation.

- **Summary**: Long-horizon coding requires agents to maintain an evolving view of the project they are changing: what problem is currently being solved, what has already been learned or modified, which other artifacts may be affected, and what work remains unresolved. - **Summary**: Machine unlearning for large language models seeks to remove specified knowledge while preserving general capability, yet current methods often treat an entire response as the unit of forgetting. - **Summary**: Although chain-of-thought prompting has improved multimodal reasoning, it is still difficult to produce correct final answers, as the reasoning can drift away from the image. - **Summary**: Assigning routine tasks to cheaper large language model (LLM) providers can reduce generation cost, but switching providers may require processing accumulated context again.
