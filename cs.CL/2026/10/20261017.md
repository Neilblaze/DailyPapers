### What Matters for NVFP4 Training? A Comparative Study of Low-Precision Pretraining Recipes
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24343v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.AI, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.24343v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-17 00:23:48+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-17 00:23:48+00:00
- **Authors**: Anjulie Agrusa, Andrei Panferov, Elizabeth Wei, Keith Wyss, Paul Gibbons, Erik Schultheis, Jinhang Choi, Frank Sun, Tijmen Blankevoort, Dan Alistarh
- **Comment**: 10 pages, 4 figures, 4 tables. Accepted at the ICML 2026 Workshop on Resource-Adaptive Foundation Model Inference (AdaptFM), Seoul, South Korea
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Training large language models directly in 4-bit floating-point (FP) formats promises substantial improvements in throughput and energy efficiency. While some recipe design choices have been validated at scale, many promising approaches remain untested beyond small models and short token horizons, leaving open the question of which trends will hold. We present a systematic comparison of recent NVFP4 recipes at medium model scale, evaluating dense 8B and 30B-A3B MoE models trained for up to 1T tokens, with a focus on which ingredients are necessary for accuracy recovery. We propose a final recipe grounded in the principles behind established stable NVFP4 training at scale, incorporating state-of-the-art techniques such as unbiased gradient estimation with lower quantization error than stochastic rounding. To the best of our knowledge, this is the strongest FP4 training result demonstrated at this scale to date in loss gap to BF16. Through ablation studies, we find that: (i) each technique in the optimized recipe measurably improves loss trajectory, (ii) selective high-precision layers are necessary for recovering accuracy at scale, (iii) not all tensors in the backward pass benefit equally from de-biasing, leaving room to apply complementary error-reduction techniques to the remaining tensors.



### FlashMap: Flow Map Speculative Decoding
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24365v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.24365v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-17 01:32:44+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-17 01:32:44+00:00
- **Authors**: Jeff Shen, Zhejian Zhou, Ryan Lee, Jonathan May, Shirley Ho
- **Comment**: Code: https://github.com/al-jshen/flashmap
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Speculative decoding accelerates large language model (LLM) inference by using a lightweight drafter to propose a block of tokens that the target model verifies in one parallel pass. Autoregressive drafters generate this block one token at a time, whereas block diffusion drafters such as DFlash generate it in one pass, making drafting faster. However, these diffusion drafters predict each token independently, ignoring dependencies between tokens in the block. We introduce FlashMap, a continuous flow drafter that conditions a flow map language model on the target model's hidden states and generates the whole block in one forward pass while capturing inter-token dependencies. On Qwen3-8B, FlashMap is faster than a matched DFlash baseline on every benchmark, with 7% higher average speedup and up to 6.1x lossless acceleration.



### Defending Against Dormant Poisoning Attacks Across Language and Multimodal Agents
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24379v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CR**, cs.CL, cs.CV
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.24379v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-17 02:26:09+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-17 02:26:09+00:00
- **Authors**: Wonjun Lee, Vaidehi Patil, Zaid Khan, Yue Zhang, Hyunji Lee, Suhyun Kim, Mohit Bansal
- **Comment**: Project page: https://wonjuun.github.io/LookAhead/
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Recent work shows that attackers can implant dormant malicious behaviors that remain hidden at release but emerge after benign fine-tuning. As foundation models are used as agents, whether dormant poisoning extends to harmful agent actions and multimodal settings remains unclear. We show two agentic risks: (1) harmful behaviors activated by benign fine-tuning can extend to unseen actions; (2) multiple harmful behaviors can coexist and be jointly activated by benign downstream fine-tuning. Existing defenses often rely on external safe responses, which are hard to obtain for agent actions, or a trusted clean reference model. We exploit a key property of dormant poisoning: the attacker must preserve safe behavior at release to conceal poisoning, and this behavior can serve as a safety reference. Leveraging this property, we automatically construct a Safety Buffer pairing harmful inputs with the released model's responses and benign versions generated by the same model to remove harmful intent. The Safety Buffer can replace external safe responses in existing defenses, but these defenses do not distinguish updates that weaken safety from those that preserve it, often increasing over-refusal or reducing utility. To address this limitation, we introduce LookAhead Defense, which previews benign fine-tuning updates using the Safety Buffer and penalizes only updates predicted to weaken safe behavior on harmful inputs or increase the likelihood of refusals on benign versions in the Safety Buffer. Across language models, language agents, and multimodal agents, LookAhead Defense reduces harmful behavior in agents from up to 93.9% to at most 14.2% and harmful responses in language models from up to 92.5% to at most 2.0% versus vanilla fine-tuning, while maintaining competitive utility and low over-refusal. It remains effective against other poisoning methods.

### Ask Less, Ask Better: Allocating User Attention in Proactive LLM Agents with Interruption Budgets
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24411v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.24411v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-17 03:23:49+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-17 03:23:49+00:00
- **Authors**: Xuanming Zhang, Amir H. Rezaeian, Miguel Ballesteros, Weiyi Sun, Werner Geyer, Lydia B. Chilton, Zhou Yu, Yassine Benajiba, Dan Roth
- **Comment**: 40 pages, 8 figures, 36 table
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: LLM agents can ask clarifying questions to complete tasks, but answering each question demands users' time and effort. Existing evaluations typically measure this burden by counting questions, treating necessary and avoidable questions equally. We propose an evaluation protocol that distinguishes how often agents ask from how well they ask. An interruption budget limits the number of questions, while a user-attention cost charges extra for questions that are hard to read or not worth asking, as rated by a human-validated LLM judge. Beyond question counts, this cost captures perceived effort: on conversation pairs with the same number of questions, human annotators agreed with the cost on which conversation required more effort 75% of the time. Across eight LLMs and four customer-service domains, limiting agents to one to three questions reduces user-attention cost by 36-73% while keeping 86-94% of the task reward they earn with unlimited questions. At the same budget, reviewing each question against the memory of users' earlier answers cuts attention cost by another 13% with little reward loss and yields more valuable questions. Models also differ sharply in how well they ask: at the same user-attention cost, the most efficient models gain more than twice as much reward from their questions as the others. These results show that asking less and asking better are distinct goals, and that evaluations of proactive agents should measure both.

- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24471v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.AI**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.24471v1)

- **Updated**: 2026-10-17 05:32:24+00:00
- **Authors**: Meng-Chen Wu, Umesh Bodhwani
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Pluralistic alignment aims to represent meaningful variation in human preferences rather than collapse heterogeneous judgments into a single target. Yet existing work often mixes three distinct questions: what pluralistic behavior a model should exhibit, whose preferences define the reference, and what evidence is sufficient to demonstrate success. We organize the literature around a two-layer taxonomy. Layer 1 distinguishes representing a range of human-supported viewpoints (Overton Pluralism), adapting faithfully to a specified perspective or preference condition (Steerable Pluralism), and matching the response distribution observed in a target population (Distributional Pluralism). Layer 2 distinguishes aggregate populations, countries and regions, and subgroups as human references. We then connect each objective to the evidence needed to support it, including perspective validity, conditional fidelity, distributional fidelity, support preservation, and uncertainty in subgroup estimates. Together, the taxonomy and evaluation framework clarify what current methods establish, where their claims exceed their evidence, and how future systems can represent disagreement without mistaking variation for faithful representation.

### Axiomatic World Modeling for Physics Reasoning
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24519v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.AI**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.24519v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-17 07:29:46+00:00

- **Authors**: Xinye Yang, Zhenyang Liu, Yuxuan Wang, Yuanyuan Lei

- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Recent research-level physics-reasoning benchmarks for LLMs reveal a failure mode that lies not in knowledge or execution, but in modeling the world being reasoned about. Models often silently alter given quantities, fabricate laws, or inject unstated assumptions to force a problem into familiar patterns, and then reason inside this corrupted world. We define this premise-level divergence as modeling drift: the gap between a model's internal axiomatic world model (AWM) and the true physical world specified in the problem. Although RLVR and PRM have yielded substantial gains in mathematics and code, their reward shapes do not directly supervise world modeling in axiomatic domains such as physics. We propose Reinforcement Learning with Axiomatic World Modeling (RLAWM), which optimizes the policy by penalizing divergence between its inferred axiomatic world model and the true physics world. RLAWM probes physics reasoning at two hierarchical levels (modeling and solving), each scored by a physically grounded reward. The modeling reward targets abstract knowledge formulation, evaluating whether the model captures the true axiomatic world before attempting calculation.
