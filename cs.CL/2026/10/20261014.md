### TopicMerge: A Divide and Conquer Approach to Topic Modeling
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19323v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.19323v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-14 00:01:35+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-14 00:01:35+00:00
- **Authors**: Matthew Raffel, Lizhong Chen, Sai Munikoti, Sameera Horawalavithana
- **Comment**: Findings of EMNLP 2026
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Recent advancements in topic modeling have split into two main paths: efficient embedding-based methods that offer scalability but are often outperformed on standard clustering metrics, and Large Language Model (LLM) approaches that achieve state-of-the-art clustering performance but at the cost of prohibitive computational expense and poor scalability. This paper introduces TopicMerge, a novel multi-stage pipeline that bridges these approaches to achieve both state-of-the-art clustering performance and computational efficiency. TopicMerge first employs an efficient clustering pipeline to deliberately over-segment a document corpus into a large set of fine-grained, semantically pure micro-clusters, which are then given initial semantic labels. It then introduces a novel, iterative merging algorithm where an LLM, guided by local neighborhood context, arbitrates on consolidating candidate pairs. These approved merges are then selectively executed and semantically relabeled by the LLM, hierarchically building broader, more coherent topics. Extensive experiments on the Bills and Wiki datasets show that TopicMerge achieves state-of-the-art clustering performance while reducing required LLM calls by over 92% compared to document-level methods, offering a practical and scalable solution.

### Counterfactual Gate-Support Decomposition: Separating Selection from Redistribution in Training-Data Filtering
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19343v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.LG**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.19343v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-14 00:36:40+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-14 00:36:40+00:00
- **Authors**: Hyojung Han, Jongmin Kim, Seung-Hun Jeon
- **Comment**: 11 pages, 2 tables, 2 figures
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: A training-data quality gate changes both which examples survive within regions of the input space and how training mass is distributed across those regions, so a gate-versus-random comparison does not isolate these mechanisms. We introduce Counterfactual Gate-Support Decomposition (CGSD), a four-arm, budget-matched design that crosses gate eligibility with support distribution and decomposes the observed gate effect into within-support selection, between-support redistribution, and their interaction. The practical constraint is finite-pool realizability: we characterize mass and token-assembly conditions under which the required arms can be constructed, together with a support-extinction case in which the ordinary design is undefined. On Korean-English and Korean-Japanese retrieval, the mean within-support selection component is the only one that exceeds its calibrated null band (+29.7 and +5.6 holdout items; 30/30 positive replicates), decisively in Korean-English and marginally in Korean-Japanese; redistribution and interaction remain unresolved at the available power. The same pattern holds for a second, unrelated gate family -- a model-confidence threshold rather than a deterministic verifier -- judged against its own null (+27.3 items, 9/9 positive), so the result is not a property of one gate's mechanism. On a mathematics gate, zero-eligibility supports leave the ordinary design undefined at every budget, while the extended design is constructible only inside narrow budget bands that an exhaustive scan locates and that the natural operating point misses -- the assembly condition separating a property of the pool from a limitation of the packer. CGSD therefore returns a decomposition, a constructible-but-underpowered design, or an explicit non-constructibility result, and says which; it prevents support redistribution from being silently interpreted as sample-level gate quality.



### Target-Token Asymmetry Can Masquerade As Depth Specialization
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19344v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.19344v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-14 00:36:45+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-14 00:36:45+00:00
- **Authors**: Danlin Li, Miao Li, Xu Han, Jiecheng Lu
- **Comment**: 48 pages, 7 figures
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Layer-ablation studies often perturb early or late layers of a language model, compare the damage to prediction on two corpora, and read a larger early-layer effect on one corpus as early-layer specialization for its task. Such a comparison averages over the tokens that each corpus asks the model to predict, and these target tokens differ: math solutions, for example, ask for far more whitespace and numbers than encyclopedia prose. We show that this difference can create the appearance of depth specialization. We split a cross-corpus early--late damage interaction by target-token type and recompute it under a target mixture shared by both corpora. Between GSM8K and Wikitext, a positive interaction replicates across five model instances. In the three fully decomposed models, only whitespace has a positive interaction. Removing whitespace reverses the pooled effect in all three, and matching the full target mixture reverses it in two. The diagnosis holds under position matching, adversarial reassignment of token types, and a continuous readout. The risk can also be anticipated from the tokenizer alone. A screen computed before any model runs places every held-out pair it scored in the region its label predicts, and on the pairs it rates at high or medium risk, removing whitespace reverses every positive pooled estimate. A smaller content-word effect one token ahead follows the share of content targets that the unablated model already predicts. A cross-corpus depth effect therefore supports an interpretation of task specialization only after the target-token mixture is reported, the effect is decomposed by token type, and a mixture-matched summary keeps its sign.

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.19359v1)

- **Authors**: Weilun Xu, Zipeng Sun
- **Comment**: 20 pages, 5 figures
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Activation steering adds a vector to a model's hidden states to control a named behaviour. A gain on that behaviour, however, does not show that the vector controls it directly: it may act through a component shared with neighbouring behaviours, which unsteered and random baselines cannot separate. We introduce the competing-target test: a vector is compared with the vector built the same way for a competing target, and an equivalence test states when their difference is below a useful margin. We apply it in open-weight models to vectors for Kantian deontology, which judges an act by the rule it follows, and act utilitarianism, which judges it by the welfare it produces. On held-out situations, prompting a framework raises its score and lowers the other's, whereas vectors for either framework move both scores alike, and none reaches the margin. The two vectors share most of their direction, a request to apply some framework; the same construction separates an optimistic from a pessimistic tone, whose vectors share much less. What a vector shares with its competitors therefore sets what it controls: steering a perspective can deliver a mixture, and measuring a disposition can measure its neighbours, unless tested against the competing target.

### Tail Competition Explains Scaling in Best-of-N Sampling for Verifiable Problems
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19362v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.AI, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.19362v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-14 01:09:16+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-14 01:09:16+00:00
- **Authors**: Taro Yano, Yoichi Ishibashi, Masafumi Oyamada
- **Comment**: Accepted at NeurIPS 2026
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Best-of-N sampling is a simple inference-time scaling strategy for large language models, yet its behavior under incomplete reward models remains poorly understood. We study Best-of-N as a competition between the extreme reward tails of correct and incorrect responses. This view shows that success depends not only on the probability of sampling a correct response, but also on whether correct responses dominate incorrect ones in the upper tail. Using extreme value theory, we characterize three asymptotic regimes: correct-tail-dominant, incorrect-tail-dominant, and critical explaining both monotonic gains and reward-hacking-induced degradation. Unlike oracle selection, convergence with an incomplete selector can be polynomial rather than exponential. Finally, we propose a lightweight tail-tension criterion to estimate when additional sampling may become harmful. Experiments on synthetic data and real reward model scores are consistent with the theoretically predicted regimes, scaling laws, and diagnostic behavior, suggesting that tail competition provides a unified lens on Best-of-N sampling for verifiable problems.
