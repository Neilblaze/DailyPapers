### No Tokenizer, Still a Gap: Cross-Lingual Fairness of Homoglyph and Invisible-Character Watermarking
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36432v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.CR, cs.CY
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.36432v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-27 00:27:01+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-27 00:27:01+00:00
- **Authors**: Alexander Nemecek, Yuqiao Xu, Joseph Falkenburg, Erman Ayday
- **Comment**: 19 pages
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Homoglyph and invisible-character watermarks are edit-based watermarks. Instead of changing how a model generates text, they hide a signal in the text after it is written. They swap some characters for homoglyphs, which look the same, or very similar, but have different Unicode code points, or they insert characters that do not display, all without access to the model that wrote the text. Generation-time watermarks work less well for lower-resourced languages due to how tokenizers split their text. Because they work on individual characters, these watermarks never touch a tokenizer, so we ask whether they escape this gap. Across 40 language varieties in 24 scripts, we measure capacity (the hidden bits each character can carry), how much of it survives routine handling and targeted removal, and how much text a keyed detector needs. Capacity varies 3.3-fold and script accounts for 96\% of that variation. Within Latin script, resource level makes no detectable difference ($ρ=+0.095$, $p=0.78$), but across scripts capacity rises with it ($ρ=+0.54$, 95\% CI $[+0.14,+0.83]$), because lower-resourced languages tend to use scripts with few homoglyphs. Ordinary copy-paste, which strips invisible characters, widens the gap: a detector then needs about 2 code points of English or Persian but 93 of Lao. Removing the tokenizer does not remove the inequity, but instead only changes its source.



### CONCUR: Concordant Auxiliary Rewards for Multi-Task, Multi-Reward Reinforcement Learning
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36437v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.36437v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-27 00:36:59+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-27 00:36:59+00:00
- **Authors**: Jixuan Leng, Si Si, Fei Wang, Cho-Jui Hsieh, Jiong Zhang, Hsiang-Fu Yu, Inderjit S. Dhillon
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: When a Large Language Model (LLM) is trained with Reinforcement Learning (RL) on multiple tasks, a task's primary reward can gate auxiliary rewards such as a length reward: on a failing response, an auxiliary cannot exceed the primary. However, group-relative advantage estimators treat a gated auxiliary like any other component, so it can reverse the primary's order, and at the primary's ceiling only auxiliaries order responses. Keeping gated auxiliaries hurts some tasks, while dropping them hurts others. We propose CONCUR, an advantage estimator that replaces the standardized scores of each gated auxiliary with the closest scores that satisfy two conditions: responses with higher primary rewards cannot receive lower scores, and responses at the primary's ceiling receive equal scores. CONCUR-T applies both conditions throughout training, and CONCUR-A lifts the second for an auxiliary that agrees with the primary's order more often than it reverses it among passing responses. On three models from two families and across math, code, instruction following, and writing, both variants score higher on average than all baselines and than when all the gated auxiliaries are dropped.



### A Heart Means Recursion: Exploring Visual Meaning and Communication in VLMs
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36449v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.AI**, cs.CL, cs.CV
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.36449v1)

- **Authors**: Alexa R. Tartaglini, Zhengxuan Wu, Thaddäus Wiedemer, Martin Wattenberg, Been Kim, Yuxuan Li
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Do vision-language models (VLMs) share our ability to communicate visually? We share preliminary findings, starting from a surprising observation: simple visual symbols like a red heart can strongly influence model behavior on seemingly unrelated tasks, such as steering models towards recursive solutions on simple coding problems. Notably, this steering effect is largely implicit, as models' direct interpretations and thinking traces about visual symbols diverge substantially from their observed behavior. In visual communication between models, this leads to a generation--interpretation gap: while models tend to send shape primitives that they (and humans) find intuitive, these visual cues often fail to steer other models. In further communication games involving abstract concepts and stories, models can creatively use visuals to transmit meaning to one another, although some communications remain non-intuitive to humans. These findings reveal that VLMs might be capable of rich, creative, and visually-grounded communication, but their interpretation and production of visual meaning diverges from human conventions in surprising ways. Together this work highlights both the promise and challenges of visual communication between humans and VLMs.

- **Authors**: Chaofei Hong, Ziyang Chen, Baochen Wang, Christina Yi Jin, Qing Zhang
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Cross-modal translation requires models to capture shared semantics across modalities while preserving the fine-grained structure required by the target modality. Yet in real-world scientific domains, paired data are often imperfect, while obtaining clean pairs at scale is costly. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) directly optimizes cross-modal translation through categorical token-level supervision, but this objective is sensitive to errors in the target sequences. JEPA offers a complementary representation-space objective, but most existing formulations focus on prediction within a modality rather than translation across modalities. We introduce Anchored Cross-Modal JEPA (AC-JEPA), an SFT-compatible plug-in that predicts masked intermediate states in both cross-modal directions using paired data. The joint-embedding objective targets cross-modal structure predictable from context, while the causal SFT path retains direct token-level supervision for the target sequence. During self-training, AC-JEPA learns from continuous latent targets derived from imperfect pseudo-labeled pairs, offering intrinsic tolerance to local data errors without promoting pseudo-tokens to hard categorical supervision. Using optical chemical structure recognition as a testbed, AC-JEPA trained on clean pairs achieves 76.07% accuracy on the Simple set of the WildMol benchmark, compared with 67.40% for SFT alone. Using the same pseudo-labeled data for both approaches, AC-JEPA-based self-training further raises accuracy to 80.23%, whereas accuracy under direct SFT drops from 67.40% to 63.47%. These results support SFT-anchored joint-embedding prediction as an effective strategy for cross-modal translation under both curated and imperfect supervision.



### Do AI Scientists Know What's New?

- **Authors**: Miri Liu, Xiayu Zhao, Encheng Xie, ChengXiang Zhai
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: A major challenge facing AI scientific discovery pipelines is how to determine when a generated idea is meaningfully novel. Ideally, novelty checks in these pipelines must be able to reject non-novel ideas while not rejecting too many novel ideas, and they should also be able to distinguish which parts of a proposal are non-novel, as opposed to opaquely discarding entire proposals. We design an analytical framework for evaluation of novelty checks with temporal ground truth at both the proposal- and claim-level, using four datasets of 1,060 proposals built from published papers, reviewer disputes, and generated ideas matching ones the field went on to publish. Because its verdict rests on claim-level evidence, ClaimNov also keeps proposals that build on established contributions, rejecting only 3% of ICLR-accepted proposals with a reviewer-disputed contribution and keeping 91 of 100 novel proposals with one spliced prior contribution, against 76 for the strongest existing check. Code and data are available at https://github.com/emmatliu/claimnov.

- **Summary**: As large language models advance rapidly, they are increasingly used to answer medical questions, and many such models are now deployed locally. Once a model is deployed, its behavior is governed mainly by the ethical policy written into its constitution. - **Summary**: Can controlled symbolic inputs reveal attention heads that influence a language model's decisions from natural language evidence?
