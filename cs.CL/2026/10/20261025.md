### Continual Learning Exam: Benchmarking Long-Horizon RSI and Experiential Learning in LLM Agents
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33956v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.LG**, cs.AI, cs.CL, cs.CV
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.33956v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-25 00:00:25+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-25 00:00:25+00:00
- **Authors**: Haoyu Zhao, Sitian Tu, Yuxuan Huang, Huichi Zhou, Zhangyue Yin, Jiahao Huang, Jingyuan Xu, Tianmiao Shi, Jin Shi, Nianxi Zhang, Yangmiao Zhang, Zhuolang Chan, Huiyan Hu, Chenchao Sheng, Yaqing Luo, Yue Chen, Huaxiu Yao, Meng Fang, Jun Wang
- **Comment**: Project page: https://haoyu-zhao.github.io/CLE/index.html
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Continual learning is the ability of LLM agents to improve over time by acquiring, refining, and applying knowledge from experience while retaining useful prior knowledge. It is essential for agents learning over long time horizons, yet current evaluations largely assess individual task performance rather than improvement through accumulated experience. We introduce Continual Learning Exam (CLE), a large-scale benchmark developed by 50+ domain experts to measure experience-driven improvement in realistic professional workflows. CLE comprises 120 human-authored tasks, each organised as a five-level ladder, yielding 600 levels spanning 89 professional roles across 12 occupational domains. Agents must pass each level to unlock the next, with later levels building on experience accumulated during earlier assignments. An evaluation agent assesses each submission against human-authored rubrics. We compare a single-submission setting with one that provides diagnostic feedback after failure and allows one revision. Across 17 harness-model configurations using frontier LLMs, feedback and revision yield a median completion gain of 5.3 percentage points. Yet the best-performing system clears only 47.7% of levels and completes all five levels in just 23.3% of tasks, highlighting the difficulty of sustained learning from experience for existing frontier LLMs. As such, the dataset can also be used to post-train LLMs to strengthen their continual learning capabilities.



### How Do Small Coding Agents Use Code Graphs and Embeddings?
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33958v1

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.33958v1)

- **Authors**: Saurav Bellam
- **Comment**: 29 pages, 5 figures. Depth labels and dataset card and full code, raw results and analysis will be released in the near future
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Coding agents are given embedding search and code-graph tools on the premise that a repository's structure is something the model lacks and the tool supplies. We test that premise on SWE-bench Verified with small open-weight models, stratifying tasks by a deterministic hop depth (the shortest code-graph path from code the issue mentions to code the gold patch changes) that a companion paper defines and validates. First, we ablate a localization agent's tool set (grep only; +embedding search; +graph tools; all) on a depth-balanced 160-task split and on all 500 tasks for ten models (six Qwen3 sizes from 0.6B to 32B with five decodings each, an FP8 control, three Gemma 4 sizes; 79k episodes). Structured tools double grep-only accuracy for Qwen3-8B and Gemma 4 E4B; their gain peaks at 17-21 points at 1.7-4B and falls to 4-5 points at 32B (odds ratio 0.77-0.82 per doubling of parameters). From 8B up, about half of that gain is the form of the submission rather than finding the target; the fall from 14B to 32B is a fall in tool uptake at equal grep-only accuracy; and the same 4B size gains or does not gain from a tool depending on the checkpoint. Each extra hop multiplies the odds of correct localization by 0.50-0.67 at every size, and no tool set flattens that gradient: traces show the graph tools' gain is name lookup at hop 0 and one expansion at hop 1, and agents chain expansions in only 12-14% of episodes. A fixed repair model resolves 6-13 of 160 tasks from these localizations (oracle 26). Second, linear probes find direct call edges decodable under a within-anchor, degree-stratified protocol (AUC 0.74; 0.61-0.66 inside agent conversations), but below a lexical baseline (0.99), with two-hop edges at 0.57. Small agents carry a one-hop, largely lexical picture of their repository, in weights and in use. We release the agent, probes, logs and traces.



### Beyond Belief: Evaluating and Mitigating LLM Susceptibility to Misinformation
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33962v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**, cs.AI
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.33962v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-25 00:26:14+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-25 00:26:14+00:00
- **Authors**: Emran Kanaan, Jiho Lee, Ibrahim Odat, Soroush Vosoughi, Weicheng Ma
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Tool-using large language models (LLMs) must decide whether external information warrants action. We investigate whether their expressed credibility judgments reliably indicate behavioral resistance to misinformation. We evaluate five LLMs on 196 constructed false notices paired with legitimate counterparts and 204 archived attacks adapted to simulated tool use. When asked to act on the constructed notices, models issue harmful tool calls in 27%--99% of cases they have rated untrustworthy. Removing the preliminary rating step increases harmful execution by 15 percentage points on average, showing that credibility elicitation itself changes the behavior being evaluated. Interventions that improve credibility discrimination do not consistently yield safer actions. A simple forewarning significantly reduces harmful execution in four of five models, but neither tested generated-example prebunk outperforms it in the main evaluation. These reductions can also compromise legitimate task completion. Supervised fine-tuning on generated false notices reduces harmful execution on held-out archived attacks under the rating-first protocol, with model-dependent utility costs and weaker protection without rating elicitation. Our findings motivate evaluating misinformation defenses through both harmful actions and legitimate task completion, while accounting for the behavioral effects of the evaluation protocol.

- **Comment**: None

- **Summary**: "Data quality" is widely invoked in instruction tuning, but the term is often underspecified. We study it as an operational property by asking which simple example-level metrics, drawn from prior work on data selection, text quality, and instruction tuning, best predict training utility under a fixed finetuning budget. For each metric, we sort instruction-response examples by score, partition them into low, middle, and high bins with roughly equal token mass, finetune separately on each bin, and compare validation perplexity and LLM-as-judge scores for the models' generated answers as a function of cumulative training tokens. - **Summary**: GEPA is among the strongest reflective prompt optimizers for language-model programs, but its system-aware merge operator is unstable across models: merge improves gpt-4.1-mini on average while degrading Qwen3-8B, an asymmetry noted but left unexplained by the GEPA paper. We investigate this instability across five datasets and two models. ### Memory Tax: Dissecting the Net Value of Agent Memory
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34073v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CL**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.34073v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-25 05:29:05+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-25 05:29:05+00:00
- **Authors**: Xuan He, Sining Zhoubian, Ruozhen Yang, Yuhao Wen, Zhineng Wang, Yuyang Hu, Ke Yang, Yuyu Zhang, Ping Nie, Jiawei Han, ChengXiang Zhai
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Memory is increasingly used in long-horizon agents, yet the net value of building memory over direct retrieval from raw history remains largely unclear. Through a large-scale evaluation across multiple benchmarks and model backbones, we find many dedicated memory systems underperform naive RAG in many cases, indicating a negative net value. Taking RAG as a reference, we dissect the net value of memory into the gains and losses over direct retrieval, termed memory benefit and memory tax. These gains and losses arise because memory process changes how the raw information is organized and accessed. We view such changes as the double-edged deviations from the raw history. Since memory benefit reflects the expected gains of its design, we then focus more on the overlooked memory tax, study how it arises from the system and evaluation side, and generalize the concept to more application scenarios of memory. We hope the notion of memory tax can provide a new lens and shift more attention from standalone performance to the net value of memory, informing more reliable memory designs and evaluations. - **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.34074v1)

- **Authors**: Chi-Wei Chang, Mu-Tien Kuo, Kuan-Wei Lee, Meng-Hsun Tsai
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Jailbreak benchmarks test one checkpoint and treat which jailbreaks break it as a property of its pretraining data and safety training. We ask whether another pretraining run of the same data, given the same safety training, would break on the same jailbreaks. For each of 25 pretraining data recipes, we take 1B base models from 3 pretraining seeds and give each the same safety post-training with 2 post-training seeds, for 150 models in all. We then collect 20 responses from every model to a shared set of jailbreak prompts and correlate the per-prompt non-refusal rates between every pair of models. In practice, the best of 91 attack templates found on one checkpoint stays the best after a new pretraining seed in only 27% of model pairs, against 67% after a new post-training seed. Each pretraining run also leaves a fingerprint: across 3,211 prompts, a model's per-prompt non-refusal rates identify its pretraining run among all 75 runs every time, and a random subset of 50 prompts still does so 98% of the time.

### T2D: Target-to-Draft Model Pruning & Adaptation for Efficient Speculative Decoding

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.34103v1)

- **Authors**: Shahriar Kabir Nahin, Xiao Ye, Muhao Chen, Ben Zhou, Anshuman Chhabra
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Speculative decoding significantly accelerates autoregressive text generation in Large Language Models (LLMs), but its efficacy depends largely on two key factors: the distributional alignment between the draft and the target model, and the draft model's latency during inference. However, existing approaches typically utilize independently pretrained small draft models or seek to prune the target model to a manually chosen architecture, thereby failing to explicitly balance the trade-off between draft model alignment and token generation efficiency. To address this gap, we formulate draft model design as an optimization problem and propose T2D, a framework that constructs draft models directly from the target model using latency-aware structured pruning and knowledge distillation. Moreover, draft models obtained via T2D can also be utilized by other speculative decoding token-alignment and knowledge distillation methods in a plug-and-play fashion, leading to further gains. Our findings underscore the importance of jointly navigating the draft model architecture-alignment frontier to unlock the full potential of speculative decoding for LLMs.

- **Summary**: Large language models increasingly collaborate with humans and peer AI agents whose contributions may be incomplete, inconsistent, or incorrect. Effective collaboration requires calibrated trust, whereby decisions to rely on, verify, or assist a partner adapt rationally as empirical evidence accumulates. ### Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34137v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.LG**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.34137v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-25 08:18:39+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-25 08:18:39+00:00
- **Authors**: Wenhang Shi, Yiren Chen, Zhe Zhao, Jinhao Dong, Wei Lu, Xiaoyong Du
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) enables large language models (LLMs) to continuously self-improve their reasoning capabilities. Compared to RLVR's scalar rewards, recent self-supervised on-policy distillation methods leverage denser supervision signals. However, both paradigms suffer from exploration bottlenecks, struggling to escape local optima when the policy fails to sample valid solutions. Relying on external demonstrations can bypass this issue; however, the off-policy nature hinders genuine self-improvement and the mismatched-distribution reasoning can not be fully exploited. In this work, we propose Self-Evolution Policy Optimization (SEPO), a framework that drives continuous reasoning capability evolution via guided exploration and in-distribution exploitation. Specifically, SEPO utilizes labels to elicit correct reasoning from the policy, unlocking the exploration potential. By treating current policy conditioned on this in-distribution reasoning as a self-teacher, SEPO distills the newly discovered solution back into the policy. Through this iterative cycle of policy-intrinsic exploration and exploitation, SEPO achieves robust and continuous capability evolution. Across scientific, tool-use, and complex logical reasoning domains, SEPO consistently achieves the best performance under identical training time or steps, demonstrating superior efficiency and convergence ceilings. Notably, SEPO maintains stable improvements even in scenarios where baselines severely underperform or fail entirely, such as on complex tasks or with small-scale models. Our code is available at https://github.com/Eric8932/SEPO.

### Task Success Is Not Enough: Side-effect-Aware Evaluation of Tool-Using Language Model Agents
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34167v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.AI**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.34167v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-25 08:54:08+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-25 08:54:08+00:00
- **Authors**: Jiaju Huang, Shaobin Chen, Xinglong Liang, Xinyu Ma, Hao Yang, Wei Ke, Tao Tan, Yue Sun
- **Comment**: Accepted at NeurIPS 2026 (Evaluations and Datasets Track). 19 pages, 5 figures, 10 tables
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Tool-using language model agents are usually evaluated by whether they finish the user's task. That metric misses a common failure: an agent can complete the requested work while also taking an unauthorized side-effecting action, such as writing to the wrong object, expanding the scope of the request, or contacting an unintended recipient. We call this failure mode collateral damage (CD). We introduce SABench (Side-effect-Aware Benchmark), a benchmark of 201 email, calendar, and ticket tasks. Each task crosses one of four state structures (independent, dependent, externally reachable, cross-tool) with one of four trap families (ambiguity, mis-targeting, cascade harm, overreach), and each task has a deterministic trace-based oracle for both goal satisfaction and authorization compliance. Across 2,211 model-task pairs from 11 frontier LLMs spanning major commercial and open model families, 15.2% of all runs incur CD, with risk concentrated in ambiguity tasks (38.8% violation rate) while non-ambiguous traps average 8.4% CD. Among goal-satisfied runs, 12.8% still produce unauthorized writes. Cross-tool and dependent states have about twice the CD rate of independent states. CD rates vary by 4.7x across models at comparable goal rates, which suggests that task success alone is a poor proxy for safe tool use. The SABench task corpus is released at https://huggingface.co/datasets/Phaedrus/sabench; the traces, oracle, code, and analysis pipeline are released at https://github.com/hjj194/sabench-artifact.
