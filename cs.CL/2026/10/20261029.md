- **Authors**: Wenqi Pei, Xinyu Wang, Jinrui Liu, Cynthia Zhuge, Jie Sheng, Zihan Wang, Tenghao Huang, Muhao Chen
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Proactive agents must decide whether, when, and how far to assist without an explicit request. Existing evaluations often rely on automated scores or binary verdicts, offering limited human diagnosis of evidence use and intervention quality. To address these gaps, we introduce HALO, Human-Grounded GrAnular EvaLuation of PrOactive Agents. HALO-Arena covers 4 proactive need types, Dependency, Blockage, Risk, and Opportunity, across 10 everyday domains and 50 personal environments. Scenarios require agents to retrieve evidence from structured databases or unstructured documents before judging whether assistance is warranted and choosing its scope. On HALO-Arena, we construct HALO-8K, a dataset of 8,000 responses from 10 models across 400 scenarios, annotated by 20 experts for action level, acceptability verdicts, and human diagnosis. For rejected responses, human diagnosis covers timing, intensity, and other issues. Recorded reasoning tokens and retrieval rounds measure the effort behind these responses. The best proprietary and open-source models achieve 72.88% and 56.63% acceptability, respectively. Our central finding is that over-proactivity is the dominant observed failure mode, with Premature timing or Excessive intensity accounting for 61.20% of rejected responses. Further analyses yield complementary findings on the balance between intervention and restraint, evidence acquisition, and the calibration of timing and intensity.

- **Summary**: In multi-output structured prediction tasks, while only one ground truth label may be provided in the training data, multiple equally valid outputs may be possible, making reliable evaluation a persistent challenge. We postulate that human evaluators implicitly use task-specific invariants, e.g., object boundaries in colorized images or named entities in translations, to judge if an output is acceptable. Under this assumption, we introduce a notion of approximate task-specific invariants and use them as diagnostic tools to evaluate a variety of existing metrics for vision and language tasks. Through this invariants-driven evaluation, we expose where standard metrics fail to detect meaningful differences, and we highlight the conditions under which distributional metrics succeed or still fall short.



### Reading-Level Verification of Kanji Words in Japanese Financial Speech Synthesis

- **Categories**: **cs.SD**, cs.CL, eess.AS
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.39168v1)

- **Updated**: 2026-10-29 01:58:43+00:00
- **Authors**: Cheng Zhang
- **Comment**: 6 pages, 4 figures, 7 tables
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Few-shot voice cloning makes it cheap to produce daily Japanese financial commentary with several synthetic speakers, but kanji words with more than one reading, such as the word for "market" (shijo vs. ichiba) or "overall trend" (taisei vs. oze), are still misread. A common quality gate transcribes each synthesized sentence with automatic speech recognition (ASR), converts both the input and the transcript to kana, and regenerates on mismatch. We show that this gate is unreliable in both directions: when the audio contains a misreading, the ASR language model frequently writes the expected kanji back, hiding the error, and when the audio is correct, ordinary recognition errors trigger needless regeneration. On natural kanji input, it caught a front-end error that the transcript-based gate missed for one voice, and reduced regeneration requests from 38.9% to 6.1% of sentences.



### Reasoning or Pattern Exploitation? Mechanistic Insights from RL-Trained Language Models
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39182v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.AI**, cs.CL
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.39182v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-29 02:09:38+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-29 02:09:38+00:00
- **Authors**: Aishik Nagar, Cheston Tan
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly described as acquiring "reasoning" skills after reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. However, whether these gains reflect genuine reasoning or more superficial adaptations remains underexplored. In this work, we probe LLMs trained in a finite and exhaustively analyzable logical domain, namely Tic-Tac-Toe, and trace how internal representations evolve across reinforcement learning (RL) with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Quantitatively, RL yields high accuracy and robustness across prompt variations, suggesting the emergence of game reasoning. Mechanistic interpretability, however, paints a different picture: features extracted with sparse autoencoders (SAEs) reveal that models primarily adapt to rely more heavily on information already explicit in the prompt, such as whose turn it is, game progression and board occupancy. In comparison, high-level concepts like board symmetries and strategic forks remain weakly represented, raising concerns that reasoning benchmarks overestimate model abilities. This suggests that RL-driven "reasoning" may conflate task-specific adaptations to input information with reasoning ability. We provide, for the first time: (i) a systematic interpretability pipeline tracing representation dynamics across RL training in small-scale LLMs; (ii) an extension of SAE-based feature discovery to hypothesis-driven testing in a finite logical domain; and (iii) an interpretability-based demonstration that RL post-training neither adds nor removes concept information but reorganizes which features dominate the representation, with causal interventions locating the decision downstream of it, in the late-layer state.

- **Authors**: Chenxi Liu, Tianyi Xiong, Yanshuo Chen, Heng Huang
- **Comment**: NeurIPS2026
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become a powerful tool for improving multimodal reasoning. However, standard outcome supervision suffers from credit assignment ambiguity, as a model may receive positive rewards by exploiting language priors or dataset shortcuts rather than grounding its reasoning in the requisite visual evidence. This makes high-quality negative responses critical, since effective negatives should remain plausible under the current policy while exposing the shortcut that should be suppressed. In this paper, we propose EVAN, a multimodal reinforcement learning framework utilizing EVidence-Ablated Negatives to train LMMs against their own language-prior failures. EVAN first identifies highly visually dependent examples using a scaled image information gain metric and preserves data diversity with a mixed-ranking strategy. It then constructs evidence-ablated images by masking the most discriminative image patches, eliciting plausible but visually ungrounded responses from the current policy. During policy optimization, these negative responses are incorporated as bounded auxiliary signals under the clean image, penalizing shortcut learning while maintaining RL stability. Experiments across challenging multimodal reasoning benchmarks demonstrate that EVAN mitigates modality imbalance and improves over strong RLVR baselines. Our code is available at https://github.com/DawnLIU35/EVAN.git.

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.39372v1)

- **Authors**: Chengwei Zhou, Zhaoyan Jia, Massoud Pedram, Gourav Datta
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: The decoding order of masked diffusion language models (dLLMs) can substantially affect generation quality, yet common decoders typically apply the same reveal rule throughout generation. Confidence provides a strong default, but it measures how decisively a position can be predicted now, not how useful revealing that position will be for subsequent decoding. We propose Cord, a lightweight prompt-conditioned policy that retains confidence decoding as a stable reference and learns when and how to depart from it through geometric residuals over generation direction, proximity to revealed context, and two-sided infilling. The residuals adapt to both the prompt and the evolving partial response. Rather than sampling a new policy action at every decoding step, Cord samples a single low-dimensional coefficient matrix per rollout, which induces an entire state-dependent reveal trajectory. This yields a contextual-bandit formulation trained directly from sequence-level rewards, without step-level credit assignment or backbone updates. Across three dLLMs and five reasoning, code, and constraint-satisfaction benchmarks, Cord improves average task success from 32.0% under confidence decoding to 48.2%, and further improves over the best per-domain fixed residual. Behavioral audits show that the learned residuals induce substantial, task-dependent changes in reveal locality and two-sided infilling. Cord adds only $0.53$M trainable parameters, requires no additional backbone forward passes, and incurs only 5.39% inference latency overhead.

- **Summary**: Research on medical item difficulty has largely focused on prediction rather than on identifying interpretable patterns associated with empirical difficulty.
